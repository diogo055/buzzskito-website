# The right photos beat more photos

**No: publishing a lot more images will not make image traffic explode**, and nothing I could read supports 50,000 or 100,000 image visits a month for this site. Your instinct about real photos was right, and it is the strongest finding here: pages that went from no photos to several jumped within days while untouched pages stayed flat, and image clicks have risen about 18-fold since September. But past about 6-8 images on a page, extra photos have added no clicks, and the true run rate is **about 1,530 image clicks per 30 days**, not 1,900, worth **about CA$23 a month once ads run** and about CA$2 until then. Websites with tens or hundreds of thousands of image visits do exist, but every one I could document is anonymous and years old, was repairing a technical block, or runs a library of 80,000 or more images. I found no named content site this size at that level and no documented case of anyone getting there by publishing more images. For July 2027, plan on **about 4,500 image clicks a month (range 2,000-10,000), worth about CA$90**, which replaces the 5,000-15,000 and CA$150-750 you were told; about 55% of today's clicks look like US school computers, and no summer image data exists at all. So the plan is cheap and led by tests: 20 minutes from you this weekend, tidying and first photos from Nov 3, coin-flip tests from Nov 16 and a real verdict in June. One spray customer equals about 81,000 image visits, so this channel is worth having cheaply, not worth taking time from leads.

Before 2013, a group of 87 publishers averaged roughly 70,000 image visits a month each, and even after Google's redesign they averaged roughly 20,000 (my arithmetic on a combined 1.4 million and 400,000 a week). A news publisher gained roughly 50,000 a month in 2019, and image search reached 30-45% of Google traffic at two e-commerce sites. "Tens of thousands" has really happened. The closest peers are the hardest evidence against the top of your hope. InsectIdentification.org gets an estimated 145,000 visits a month from every channel combined, and BugGuide about 750,000 with 2.16 million images, or about 0.35 visits per image ([Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/website/insectidentification.org/); [Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/website/bugguide.net/)). So 100,000 image clicks a month would match the entire traffic of the closest topical peer. These estimates are shaky for small sites (What's That Bug ranges from about 56,000 to 545,000 depending on the tool) and none of them shows an image split ([documented cases](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/documented_image_traffic_cases.md)). Per image we are already doing well, at about 4 clicks per distinct image a month from image search alone.

No case shows that publishing more images caused the traffic. The largest documented gain came from removing a block. The biggest swings came from Google's own design: down 63% on average in 2013, up an average 37% in 2018. Controlled tests of on-page image changes were inconclusive, such as +7.3% with a range of -4.2% to +19.4% on alt text ([SearchPilot](https://www.searchpilot.com/resources/case-studies/does-adding-descriptive-alt-text-to-listing-images-improve-seo)). Big percentages elsewhere hide small bases: one agency's +534% impressions was 3 more clicks ([May-Fly](https://www.may-fly.co.uk/alt-text-for-images-case-study/)). The largest multi-site study put Images at about 12% of impressions and under 1% of clicks across 200+ enterprise domains ([seoClarity, 2019](https://www.seoclarity.net/blog/seo-research-real-traffic-share)). You sit at about 14% of Google clicks, well above that norm and well below Kohn's shops.

My answer to how much a site like this can really get: 2,000-10,000 Image-tab clicks a month is plausible by next summer, 10,000 is a stretch, 30,000 has no precedent at this size that I could read, and 100,000 has none since 2013.

## People talk about Lens, but almost nobody publishes image clicks

Google's numbers are huge and say nothing about clicks. The newest is over 50 billion visual searches a month (Sundar Pichai, Oct 28, 2026), not labelled as Lens and with no definition ([Google](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/message-ceo/alphabet-earnings-q3-2026/)). The often repeated 25 billion Lens figure was published in June 2026 but is footnoted to April 2025 data, so the two cannot be compared ([Google](https://business.google.com/en-all/think/search-and-video/ai-search-marketing-customer-journey-india/)). The claim that 1 in 5 Lens searches is commercial comes from Google's advertising side and covers all Lens use, so it says nothing about a photo of a spider. No official figure counts clicks to websites or covers the Images tab.

Lens only became measurable on Sep 24, 2026, when Search Console added a Web: Multimodal filter covering Lens, Circle to Search, image uploads and Chrome's image search ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2026/09/web-multimodal-in-sc?hl=en)). It has no search terms and cannot be pulled automatically, and Google's John Mueller says this traffic was not in the earlier counts ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-search-console-multimodal-search-type-filter-42156.html)). Only two named sources have published numbers, one site each: about 0.2% of text-search impressions at a 0.5% click rate, and about 2% at about 1% ([SEO-Kreativ](https://www.seo-kreativ.de/en/blog/search-console-multimodal-en/); [Linus Li](https://www.linusseo.com/en/gsc-web-multimodal/)). Our only reading is 34 Lens clicks against 117 image clicks in one week around Oct 24 ([site note](../app/image-sitemap.xml/route.ts)). That is one week, but it hints that a bug site may do better here than an SEO blog, and your export will settle it.

Recognised experts mostly hedge. Glenn Gabe (June 2025) is the clearest supporter: image clicks and impressions stayed in step while web search came apart, which he offers as a partial offset to AI losses, with the warnings that image traffic is far below web and this may not last ([GSQi](https://www.gsqi.com/marketing-blog/the-forgotten-coupling-image-search-video-mode-and-news-tab-in-search/)). Kevin Indig (January 2026) notes the billions of Lens searches and says plainly that nobody knows where those clicks go ([Advanced Web Ranking](https://www.advancedwebranking.com/blog/beyond-old-seo-models-ai-search)). Lily Ray argues that in AI search an image becomes material for the answer, not a route to a website ([Lily Ray](https://lilyraynyc.substack.com/p/a-reflection-on-seo-and-ai-search)). Mueller said in 2022 that searching visually is rare in many areas ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-searching-visually-is-rare-34193.html)), while Google's Gary Illyes called Images overlooked by publishers back in 2019 ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://seroundtable.com/media-search-overlooked-by-publishers-google-27100.html)). The confident numbers, such as 20-40% of organic traffic from images, come from vendor pages and trace to no data ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/image-seo-2026-visual-search-optimization-guide)).

I found no public report of a sudden Image-tab jump in 2026 like yours, lasting or reversing. The October threads I could read talk about Discover, News, bots and ranking swings ([WebmasterWorld](https://www.webmasterworld.com/google/5134184.htm); [Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-search-ranking-volatility-42379.html)), and the biggest forums were unreadable. The fair answer to your question is that this is under-reported, not undiscovered. Silence fits a hidden channel and it fits a channel too small to bother with, and public evidence cannot separate the two.

AI answers are the main threat I can see. In a randomised experiment with 1,065 US desktop users, AI Overviews cut outbound organic clicks by 38% in a working paper ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ai-overviews-cut-organic-clicks-38-field-study-finds/573145/)), and a second experiment with 1,100 adults found AI Mode cut click-through by 18.8 points ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352)). Neither isolates visual searches. An ID search looks exposed, because the reader wants a name and Google can give it on the spot. In 15 of 16 pest searches an image block sits at the top of normal results, mostly inside the AI Overview, and BuzzSkito was in none of about 155 slots, even on chigger bites where it is #5 in the Images tab ([teardown](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/pest_image_search_teardown.md)). Google also mixed ads into image results in June ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-image-search-mix-ads-41511.html)) and added a browsable Images home and image creation in AI Overviews in July ([Google](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/google-images-25th-anniversary/)), with no measured effect yet. This channel can be cut from above, as 2013 showed.

Among bite, spider and ID pages, clicks per page peak at 6-8 images (0.69-1.28 a weekday), then fall to 0.40 at 9-10 and 0.04-0.11 at 11-15. Be careful with that fall: the 11+ group is mostly droppings and broad "tiny bugs" pages, so the honest claim is no gain, not harm. With page type, topic, age and web strength held equal, 6-10 images against 3-5 gives x1.4-2.4 impressions and x1-3 clicks with wide ranges, and on older pages with their earlier traffic held equal, 6-10 is no better than 3-5 ([own-site test](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/own_site_image_test.md)). Google's counting supplies a reason: one page counts once per search, so an extra image only helps if it catches a new search ([Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7042828)). Outside this site, I found no controlled test showing that more images raise image traffic, and a 2021 review found no evidence that image count is a ranking factor ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ranking-factors/image-quantity/)).

Where volume does work is breadth, and this is your best evidence. From Oct 17 to Oct 25, distinct images rose x2.2, impressions x2.4 and clicks x2.5. Allowing for the x1.41 lift on pages with no new images, new images brought about x1.7 impressions for x2.2 images, which is close to proportional. Two limits apply. The best topics went first, so later pages will probably yield less, and "more" has to mean new files: 661 distinct images fill 2,257 placements, and the same file on another page gives Google nothing new. The strongest untested argument for your idea is Lens. A photo someone takes of an unknown bug is on no website, so Lens can only match it by looks, which favours several life stages, angles and phone-style shots ([publishing notes](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/does_publishing_more_images_work.md)). The November test measures Lens clicks per page too.

### Format and rank are the open prize: we hold 2 of 240 top slots

The field is wide open. Across 16 searches, 149 different sites hold the 240 top-15 slots, pest firms and brands are the biggest group at 36%, and no stock library or Pinterest page reaches the top 15. By site type, about 47% of slots look beatable and 45% look hard. BuzzSkito holds 2: chigger bites at #5 and baby cockroach at #14. It appears in the first 100 on 7 searches and is absent on 9, including all five spider-name searches and tick ([teardown](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/pest_image_search_teardown.md)). A warning on Search Console's rosy positions: it shows 2.1 for black widow spider and 1.2 for tick, but a browser check found us nowhere in the first 100 on those searches, so do not count them as top slots.

Length and photo count are not what holds us back. Cleveland Clinic is #1 on chigger bites with 1,725 words and one image, against our 8,491 words and six images at #5, and McCall's 624-word page with one stock photo sits ten places above us on baby cockroach ([teardown](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/pest_image_search_teardown.md)). Winning images fit the search. Bite searches reward real skin with many bites. "Vs" searches reward one image holding both things, with at least 9 of the top 15 on tick vs bed bug doing this. Droppings and nymph searches reward a coin or finger for scale, and species searches reward a clean whole-body shot or a stunning macro. Tall labelled cards hold 3 of 16 #1 slots ([chigger bites](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&gl=us&hl=en&q=chigger+bites); [tick vs bed bug](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&gl=us&hl=en&q=tick+vs+bed+bug); [mouse droppings](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&gl=us&hl=en&q=mouse+droppings)). That is why these formats are a pilot below, not a rollout.

Aim first at baby cockroach (#14), tick vs bed bug (#23), house spiders (#26) and bugs that look like ticks (#31), where small firms with one cheap image hold most slots. Defend chigger bites (#5). Leave the single-name searches for black widow, tick, jumping spider, wolf spider and brown recluse alone for now, because Wikipedia, universities and national brands hold 7-12 of their 15 slots. The most reachable placement I saw is the classic Images pack at the very top of normal results for baby cockroach, 11 slots with us at #14, but packs click poorly: published readings run from about 0.01% to 0.2% ([GSQi](https://www.gsqi.com/marketing-blog/image-pack-rankings-in-google-web-search/)). ### Original photos help less than the project note says, and almost nothing is measured

The project note says unique original photos are what win Google Images. The evidence does not support that as a rule, and it does not refute it either. On the side of originals: Mueller said in 2022 that a stock image on 20-30 other sites would not rank in Image Search, though it would not count against the page ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-on-use-of-stock-photography/468849/)), and the one controlled test, on 10 new sites with an invented keyword in 2019, preferred unique images without reporting a size ([Reboot Online](https://www.rebootonline.com/blog/long-term-duplicate-image-experiment/)). CDC's own page is #14 on mouse droppings with what appears to be a photo we also use, and our copy did not appear in the first 100. On tick vs bed bug, our photos with 0 and 8 copies elsewhere appeared, and our CDC bed bug shot with 297 copies did not.

Against it: **10 of the 12 top images I could check were stock or widely reused**, including a #1 flea photo with 37 copies and a #3 wolf spider photo with 84. That is a small sample, TinEye can miss copies, and page strength is not held equal ([teardown](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/pest_image_search_teardown.md)). Mueller also said in 2021 that uniqueness is not the same as quality ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-images-uniqueness-higher-quality-32053.html)). Our CDC nymph photo reached #14 with its source not in the first 60. Pages whose only images are reused photos made 36% of October's image impressions and 159 of 402 clicks ([publishing notes](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/does_publishing_more_images_work.md)). And most of our CDC photos are not widely copied: of 8 checked, only the bed bug shot is common.

Diagrams are the most interesting signal. On 5 of the 6 pages with a diagram that appeared in the snapshots, the diagram was the page's best-ranked image. That comparison holds the page's strength equal, which most evidence here does not. Our bite-pattern diagram is the #5 on chigger bites. But pages with a diagram did not beat pages without one overall (median 220 impressions against 868, muddied by topic and age), and whether diagrams earn clicks as well as rank is unknown. Search Console cannot show clicks per image.

So weigh it this way: originality is a tie-breaker, not a gate. Prefer photos few others use, do not expect to outrank the source with a famous one, and treat a new labelled comparison or our own diagram as a new image. The whole evidence base is one 2019 lab test, 12 images checked on TinEye, single snapshots and a few employee remarks. Nobody has measured the advantage of an original photo in real, competitive image results. Technician photos in May would be the first such measure I know of.

Ads are worth about 2 cents a visit at the CA$20 per 1,000 sessions estimated for Nov-Dec. Image visitors are about 85% US and US traffic pays most, which adds about 20%. Ads are not live yet, so until they are, only the Amazon part is real: about CA$2 a month.

Published evidence is thin. ### What Google's scraping policy means here

Google's spam policy counts it as scraping when a site exists mainly to gather other people's images and offers them "without substantial added value" ([Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)). The same page warns against patching pages together from other sources and against near-identical pages built to catch searches. That is the wording closest to a site whose 720 ledger photos all come from elsewhere: iNaturalist 385, Wikimedia Commons 206, CDC 125, National Park Service 4. It does not describe the guides as they stand, since each has real writing, sources and a fact-check around the photos. I found no named publisher with before-and-after data hurt by mass image publishing in 2023-2026 ([publishing notes](../research_notes/Google%20Images%20traffic%20growth%20plan/does_publishing_more_images_work.md)), so the risk rests on policy wording and on lessons like WordPress switching off attachment pages by default in 2023 ([WordPress](https://make.wordpress.org/core/2023/10/16/changes-to-attachment-pages/)). In plain terms, real photos on real guides are low risk, and new thin pages built around images are where the danger sits. The worst patterns are one URL per photo, galleries, and state or species pages whose main difference is swapped public photos. Mueller adds a practical reason: a gallery of 50 images does not read as the landing page for any one of them, and a photo deserves its own page only with unique text to go with it ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-unique-image-landing-pages-can-help-boost-search-visibility/553282/)).

The tick photo is the live example. CDC's shot of a blacklegged tick adult and nymph on a fingertip ([CDC PHIL 28383](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/phil/Details.aspx?pid=28383), public domain) sits on 48 of our pages, 29 of them state and province pages. I found no measured harm. It is among our appearances on tick vs bed bug, TinEye shows only 8 copies elsewhere, and a comparison of widely reused and barely reused images found no penalty, though the widely reused ones are also the tick and bed bug photos with the most demand, so that comparison cannot settle it. Four of the six multi-image pages that did not show at all in Oct 22-29 are tick state pages carrying it, but 25 other state and province pages carrying it did show. The real problem is different. The 48th placement gave Google nothing new to index and makes it choose among our own pages. And state pages average 6.3 images, of which 5.5 appear on 10 or more pages, which is the swapped-photos look the policy describes, on pages earning 1.2 image clicks a month. The possible downside is site-wide and the upside is pennies.

So add this photo and the other heavily used tick photos to no new page. From now on, cap new placements of any photo at about 10 pages, which is my judgement and not a measured limit. Leave the photo where it earns, such as tick-vs-bed-bug and types-of-ticks-identification. When a state page is edited for another reason, swap one or two of its most reused photos for a tick photographed in that state. Do not mass-swap now, because it would change image addresses across dozens of pages during the tests, and Google has said replaced image URLs take a long time to be picked up ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/why-updating-images-can-backfire/445977/)), even though new photos here have shown within days.

## Conclusion

October looked like a flood but behaved like a door. The site had no real photos, and adding them opened a gate that can only open once: 372 of 789 pages already have photos, and most of what remains lacks visual demand. From here, growth comes from taking better slots in a field where we hold 2 of 240 top positions and where a one-photo, 624-word page can beat us, so the next gains are about fit and placement, not quantity. The surprise in the evidence is that the channel's biggest audience may be classrooms, and the number that decides its future is not the headline but the school-free 680 clicks a month and how far that grows by June.

The most valuable image visitor is missing from every average here. A US student on a spider page is worth a fraction of a cent to a cent or two, while a GTA homeowner who finds a tick photo in June could become a customer worth 81,000 of those visits, and 55 of about 70 local mosquito and tick articles still have no real photo. Leads per 1,000 sessions ran 40 in May and 0.3 in September ([your season rule](C:/Users/buzzs/.claude/projects/C--Users-buzzs-buzzskito-website/memory/feedback_season_data.md)), so nothing measured in October can say whether that visitor exists, and quote requests by landing page next spring will. If that link shows up, image search stops being a rounding error in the ad report and starts mattering to the business. If it does not, you will have spent almost nothing to learn it, and the photos will still be doing their job for readers.
