# Visual guides: where the artwork and the sources disagree

For the owner. Each of the ten visual guides opens with the one-page infographic you supplied.
Every claim on each graphic was checked against government, university and veterinary sources.
Where a source says something different, **the page text states the sourced fact** and carries a
short note titled "Where the one-page guide differs", so the published pages are correct today.
The artwork itself was not edited. This list is what to change if you re-export the graphics,
which matters most for the copies pinned on Pinterest, because a pin travels without the page.

## /blog/fall-mouse-proofing-checklist

Graphic: `public/guides/bz-guide-03-mouse-proofing.webp`

Fact-check of the page text: 95 claims checked, 10 corrected, 6 removed or softened.

Source of this list: fact-check.

1. **The graphic says:** 1. Gaps around pipes: "the #1 mouse door."
   **The sources say:** No source ranks entry points. Nebraska says common openings occur around pipes and electrical conduits or cables; the CDC lists pipes among places to check.
   Source: https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1530/html/view
2. **The graphic says:** 2. Under exterior doors: "If light shows, mice fit."
   **The sources say:** The limit is a measurement, not daylight: the door-to-threshold gap should be less than 1/4 inch (Nebraska), and a mouse can squeeze under a gap 1/4 inch tall (UC IPM). Light shows through thinner gaps.
   Source: https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1530/html/view
3. **The graphic says:** 3. Dryer & exhaust vents: "Unscreened vents are open invitations."
   **The sources say:** Attic and crawl space vents get hardware cloth, but a clothes dryer vent should not be screened. Nebraska: improper screening hastens lint build-up and increases the likelihood of fires. DOE job aid: termination has a backdraft damper and no cage, and does not include a grille, cage or screen (IRC 1502.3).
   Source: https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-07/17-1_vent-clothes-dryer.pdf
4. **The graphic says:** 4. Foundation cracks: "Even hairline cracks widen with frost."
   **The sources say:** None of the cited sources says frost widens cracks enough to admit mice. A hairline crack is under the 1/4 inch a mouse needs; the documented risk is gnawing (do not ignore smaller crevices, as gnawing can quickly enlarge them). Repair with concrete or masonry grout.
   Source: https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1530/html/view
5. **The graphic says:** THE 1/4-INCH RULE: "Seal anything wider than 6 mm."
   **The sources say:** A hole of 1/4 inch (6 mm) is itself enough: CDC says the width of a pencil, 1/4 inch or 6 mm in diameter; UC IPM says openings as small as 1/4 inch; Nebraska says holes as small as 1/4 inch in diameter and not to ignore smaller crevices. The artwork should say 6 mm or wider.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/rodent-control/seal-up.html
6. **The graphic says:** SEAL IT RIGHT: "Pack coarse steel wool tight into the gap. Cover with silicone caulk or cement. Mice can't chew through the metal fibers."
   **The sources say:** UC IPM does say mice can't chew through coarse steel wool, but it and Nebraska call steel wool a temporary plug that rusts; Nebraska names copper or stainless steel wool for openings under 3/4 inch. The CDC says to put caulk around the steel wool (no source names silicone) and lists cement for larger holes, not as a cover for steel wool.
   Source: https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1530/html/view
7. **The graphic says:** Footer: "Never use poison indoors — mice die in walls."
   **The sources say:** The reason is supported (UC IPM: rodenticide use will often result in mice dying inside the structure), but 'never' is not: the EPA says consumer ready-to-use bait stations are labeled for use indoors, or indoors and outdoors within 50 feet of buildings, and the CDC says to use poison or bait stations only for infestations that persist.
   Source: https://www.epa.gov/rodenticides/restrictions-rodenticide-products

## /learn/ant-killer-recipes

Graphic: `public/guides/bz-guide-01-ant-recipes.webp`

Source of this list: writer (fact-check not finished).

1. **The graphic says:** Title: "4 tested recipes with exact ratios".
   **The sources say:** Nothing shows these four were tested. Recipe 1's ratio is not the one any university publishes, and recipe 2 has no published household recipe at all. The page avoids the word and names who publishes each ratio. Suggested wording: "4 recipes checked against university sources".
   Source: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/ant-management-in-gardens-and-landscapes/
2. **The graphic says:** 1. Borax Sugar Bait: 1 tsp borax : 3 tsp sugar : 2 tbsp warm water.
   **The sources say:** University of California: 1/2 tsp boric acid powder + 9 tsp sugar in 1 cup hot water (about 1% boric acid, 19% sugar). Effective range is 0.5 to 1% boric acid and 10 to 25% sugar; above 1% is less effective because it repels ants. The graphic works out to 8 tsp powder per cup of water, 16 times the UC recipe. Two University of Hawaii extension handouts are similarly weak (1 tsp boric acid + 3 tbsp sugar, water to 1 3/4 cups; 1 tsp boric acid + 1 cup sugar + 3 cups water). Texas A&M also says 1% beats higher concentrations.
   Source: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/ant-management-in-gardens-and-landscapes/
3. **The graphic says:** 1. Borax Sugar Bait uses borax as the measured ingredient.
   **The sources say:** Every university recipe with kitchen measures uses boric acid powder (UC, Texas A&M, University of Hawaii). UC says the bait can be made with boric acid or borax but gives no separate borax measure, and no source converts one to the other. Either relabel the ingredient as boric acid powder or drop the borax amount to the weak recipe.
   Source: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/ant-management-in-gardens-and-landscapes/
4. **The graphic says:** 1. Borax Sugar Bait: "soak cotton balls, set in bottle caps along ant trails", while its own safety tag says "enclosed stations only".
   **The sources say:** The picture and the tag contradict each other. UC says bait stations protect bait from disturbance by children and should not be in areas accessible to pets and small children. Texas A&M does use bottle caps, foil and straw pieces, but says never to place any bait in areas accessible to small children or pets. The artwork should show an enclosed station.
   Source: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/ants/
5. **The graphic says:** 1. Safety tag MODERATE: "Borax is toxic if eaten".
   **The sources say:** NPIC rates boric acid as low in toxicity if eaten or on skin, though swallowing it has caused nausea, vomiting, stomach aches and diarrhea, and signs in animals can start within 2 hours. It adds a hazard the tag omits: borax can be corrosive to the eye and irritating to skin. Children may be especially sensitive. The keep-away instruction is right; better wording would be "harmful if swallowed, eye irritant".
   Source: https://npic.orst.edu/factsheets/boricgen.html
6. **The graphic says:** 2. Baking Soda + Sugar: 1 tbsp baking soda : 1 tbsp powdered sugar, sprinkled in shallow lids near entry points; reapply after 3 days.
   **The sources say:** No extension or government source I opened lists baking soda for ants or gives a ratio or reapply interval. The only evidence found is a lab study (Brinkman & Gardner 2004, Florida Entomologist 87(3):324-329): ants confined on baking-soda-dusted surfaces died over about 6 days (89.5% of Argentine ants at 28 mg/cm2, over 99% of both species at 152 mg/cm2), but in sugar water only about 50% of Argentine ants died after 6 days and stronger mixes did no better. No home or field trial. The page gives no recipe block for it.
   Source: https://complete.bioone.org/journals/florida-entomologist/volume-87/issue-3/0015-4040(2004)087[0324:MOAHFP]2.0.CO;2/MORTALITY-OF-ANT-HYMENOPTERA--FORMICIDAE-PEST-SPECIES-EXPOSED-TO/10.1653/0015-4040(2004)087[0324:MOAHFP]2.0.CO;2.full
7. **The graphic says:** 2. Safety tag LOW: "Food-grade ingredients - still keep lids out of pet reach".
   **The sources say:** The tag mentions pets only. Poison Control says swallowing too much baking soda can cause serious toxicity (vomiting and diarrhea, then possibly seizures, dehydration and kidney failure) and says to keep it out of reach of children. NPIC adds that even too much of a low-toxicity ingredient can be harmful. The tag should name children too.
   Source: https://www.poison.org/articles/baking-soda
8. **The graphic says:** 3. Vinegar Trail-Breaker: "Spray directly on trails, entry points and nests".
   **The sources say:** The 1:1 ratio is supported: University of Nebraska-Lincoln says to wipe down surfaces with a 1:1 vinegar and water mix to remove pheromone trails and deter ants. Nothing supports spraying nests. Penn State lists vinegar and water only for wiping trails, and names hot soapy water, not vinegar, for nests. Remove "and nests".
   Source: https://byf.unl.edu/ants/
9. **The graphic says:** 3. Safety tag LOW: "Safe around kids & pets".
   **The sources say:** Broader than the sources. ASPCA says vinegar is acidic and can cause stomach upset and irritation, though it is not generally an issue once diluted, rinsed and dry; undiluted it can cause vomiting, diarrhea, oral irritation and pain. Poison Control says vinegar in the eyes commonly causes irritation and redness and to keep vinegar away from children. Suggested wording: "Lower risk when diluted; wipe dry, keep out of eyes".
   Source: https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/poisonous-household-products
10. **The graphic says:** 4. Dish Soap Knockdown: "Breaks surface tension - kills on contact".
   **The sources say:** Kill on contact is supported (UC: some soap products can kill ants on contact with no residual toxicity). The mechanism is not: Nebraska Extension says insecticidal soaps may disrupt cell membranes or remove the insect's protective wax so it dies of water loss, and that the spray must wet the insect.
   Source: https://lancaster.unl.edu/washing-pests-away/
11. **The graphic says:** 4. Safety tag LOW: "Safe once dry".
   **The sources say:** No source uses that phrase. UC and Nebraska Extension say soap leaves no residual toxicity or activity, which means it stops killing ants once dry. Poison Control says swallowing a small amount of dish soap can cause nausea, vomiting or diarrhea, that it irritates eyes, and to store it out of sight of children. NPIC notes dish soap can contain additives that could cause harm. Suggested wording: "Lower risk; rinse surfaces, store the bottle out of reach".
   Source: https://www.poison.org/articles/hand-dish-soap

## /blog/signs-of-bed-bugs-checklist

Graphic: `public/guides/bz-guide-02-bedbug-signs.webp`

Fact-check of the page text: 106 claims checked, 7 corrected, 6 removed or softened.

Source of this list: fact-check.

1. **The graphic says:** 3. Shed skins - pale yellow empty shells, ~1mm.
   **The sources say:** The EPA's 'about 1mm' applies to eggs and eggshells, not skins. Skins are shed by nymphs the EPA sizes at 1.5 mm (first stage) to 4.5 mm (fifth stage), about 1/16 to 3/16 inch, so skins vary in size. 'Pale yellow' and 'molt 5 times' are supported (EPA, University of Kentucky).
   Source: https://www.epa.gov/bedbugs/bed-bugs-appearance-and-life-cycle
2. **The graphic says:** 5. Tiny white eggs - grain-of-rice sized.
   **The sources say:** The EPA says eggs are about 1 mm, the size of a pinhead, pearl-white. Kentucky says about the size of a dust speck and hard to see without magnification. A grain of rice is several times longer. 'Sticky' is supported (Kentucky: sticky when first laid).
   Source: https://www.epa.gov/bedbugs/bed-bugs-appearance-and-life-cycle
3. **The graphic says:** 6. Musty sweet odor - coriander-like smell in heavy infestations. LOOK: whole room - trust your nose.
   **The sources say:** CDC says 'a sweet musty odor', the EPA 'musty-sweetish', Kentucky a 'buggy' odor. None of the cited sources uses coriander (a search found it only in trade and pest-company writing). Kentucky says the smell is seldom apparent except in extreme infestations and should not be relied upon for detection, which contradicts 'trust your nose'.
   Source: https://entomology.mgcafe.uky.edu/ef636
4. **The graphic says:** 7. Bite lines - clusters or zigzags on exposed skin overnight.
   **The sources say:** CDC says bite marks may be random or appear in a straight line. No cited source describes clusters or zigzags.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/bed-bugs/about/index.html
5. **The graphic says:** 7. Bite lines - LOOK: arms, neck, ankles - morning check.
   **The sources say:** CDC says most people do not notice marks until one to several days after the bite, and marks may take as long as 14 days in some people, so a morning check can miss them. The EPA calls bites a poor indicator. CDC lists face, neck, arms, hands; Kentucky says fleabites occur mainly around the ankles while bed bugs feed on any exposed skin. Suggest replacing 'ankles' with 'face' or 'hands'.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/bed-bugs/about/index.html
6. **The graphic says:** Footer: Found 2+ signs?
   **The sources say:** No source uses a count of signs. Kentucky says confirmation requires finding the bugs and/or their signs, often by a professional. One identified bug is enough; bites plus odor (two signs) are not.
   Source: https://entomology.mgcafe.uky.edu/ef636
7. **The graphic says:** Footer: Call a pro for heat treatment.
   **The sources say:** Heat is one legitimate professional method (Kentucky: air heated to about 120 to 130 F, often one day), not the only one. CDC says professional companies will typically spray insecticides. Suggest 'Call a pro' without naming one method.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/bed-bugs/about/index.html

## /learn/tick-identification-chart

Graphic: `public/guides/bz-guide-04-tick-chart.webp`

Fact-check of the page text: 90 claims checked, 8 corrected, 8 removed or softened.

Source of this list: fact-check.

1. **The graphic says:** ACTUAL SIZE (unfed adults): Deer tick = poppy seed.
   **The sources say:** The poppy seed is the nymph. CDC: adult blacklegged ticks are approximately sesame-seed size and nymphs approximately poppy-seed size. Handbook: unfed adult female 3 to 3.7 mm (about 1/8 inch), nymph 1.3 to 1.7 mm.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/lyme/causes/index.html
2. **The graphic says:** ACTUAL SIZE (unfed adults): Lone star = sesame seed (drawn a seed class larger than the deer tick).
   **The sources say:** No cited source compares the lone star tick to a sesame seed. The handbook puts it at about 3 to 4 mm, practically the same as an adult female blacklegged tick (3 to 3.7 mm), which is the tick CDC calls sesame-seed size. The two adults should be shown about the same size.
   Source: https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/caes/documents/publications/bulletins/b1010pdf
3. **The graphic says:** ACTUAL SIZE (unfed adults): Dog tick = apple seed.
   **The sources say:** No cited source uses an apple seed for the American dog tick. The handbook gives adults as almost 6.4 mm (1/4 inch).
   Source: https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/caes/documents/publications/bulletins/b1010pdf
4. **The graphic says:** Engorged (fed) ticks swell to 3x this size.
   **The sources say:** No source gives 3x. The one measured case, the female American dog tick, goes from almost 6.4 mm to as much as 13 mm (about double in length); a fed blacklegged female ends nearly as large from half the starting length; male dog ticks do not change notably in size. At best a rough rule for females only.
   Source: https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/caes/documents/publications/bulletins/b1010pdf
5. **The graphic says:** Black-legged (deer) tick LOOKS LIKE: Reddish body, black legs.
   **The sources say:** The reddish body describes the unfed female (reddish body, dark brown shield). The male is smaller (2 to 2.7 mm) and completely dark brown.
   Source: https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/caes/documents/publications/bulletins/b1010pdf
6. **The graphic says:** Black-legged tick DISEASES: Lyme disease + anaplasmosis.
   **The sources say:** Correct but incomplete. CDC lists six: anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Lyme disease, hard tick relapsing fever, a form of ehrlichiosis and Powassan virus disease.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/about/where-ticks-live.html
7. **The graphic says:** Black-legged tick ONTARIO RANGE: Widespread across southern Ontario.
   **The sources say:** Government of Canada lists it as established in west, south and southeast Ontario, a wider footprint than the south alone.
   Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/diseases/ticks-tick-borne-diseases/ticks.html
8. **The graphic says:** American Dog tick DISEASES: Rocky Mountain spotted fever (rare in ON).
   **The sources say:** CDC lists tularemia as well as Rocky Mountain spotted fever. 'Rare in Ontario' fits the record: not reportable in Ontario, one documented locally acquired case (1978, near Ottawa).
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/about/where-ticks-live.html
9. **The graphic says:** American Dog tick ONTARIO RANGE: Grassy fields & trails province-wide.
   **The sources say:** Habitat matches. 'Province-wide' goes too far: the Public Health Ontario study places it in the southern portions of Ontario, with established populations in 31 of 35 health units by 2018 and the North East region mostly without established populations; it is still spreading.
   Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-15009-9
10. **The graphic says:** Lone Star tick DISEASES: Ehrlichiosis + alpha-gal (red-meat allergy).
   **The sources say:** Correct but incomplete. CDC also lists tularemia, Heartland virus, Bourbon virus and STARI (a circular rash of unknown cause).
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/about/where-ticks-live.html
11. **The graphic says:** Lone Star tick ONTARIO RANGE: Moving into southern Ontario.
   **The sources say:** Government of Canada says lone star ticks 'have been found' in the southern regions of six provinces including Ontario; unlike the blacklegged and American dog ticks it is not described as established.
   Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/diseases/ticks-tick-borne-diseases/ticks.html
12. **The graphic says:** IF BITTEN: Watch 30 days for rash, fever, fatigue; see a doctor if any appear.
   **The sources say:** CDC's current page says to see a doctor for a rash or fever within several weeks of removing a tick; it does not name fatigue or 30 days. The 30-day watch is the 2007 Connecticut handbook's figure.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/after-a-tick-bite/index.html
13. **The graphic says:** TICK-CHECK step 2: armpits, groin, hairline, behind ears, waistband (five areas).
   **The sources say:** CDC lists seven areas: under the arms, in and around the ears, inside the belly button, back of the knees, in and around the hair, between the legs, around the waist. The graphic omits the belly button and the backs of the knees.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html
14. **The graphic says:** TICK-CHECK step 3: Tumble dry clothes on HIGH 10 min, kills missed ticks.
   **The sources say:** CDC's 10 minutes on high heat is for dry clothing; damp clothes may need additional time, and if washing first, hot water is recommended because cold and medium temperature water will not kill ticks.
   Source: https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention/index.html

## /learn/pet-safe-pest-control

Graphic: `public/guides/bz-guide-05-pet-safe.webp`

Fact-check of the page text: 96 claims checked, 6 corrected, 9 removed or softened.

Source of this list: fact-check.

1. **The graphic says:** Borax baits: Cats UNSAFE, Dogs UNSAFE. Toxic if eaten.
   **The sources say:** NPIC rates boric acid as low in toxicity if eaten or on skin (borax can be corrosive to the eye); signs in animals can start within 2 hours. ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center veterinarians wrote that serious toxicosis is not expected from ant and roach baits because of the low concentration and small bait size, signs are usually limited to mild GI upset, and the plastic or metal housing (obstruction) is often the greater concern. The chart's advice on enclosed, out-of-reach stations is supported.
   Source: https://npic.orst.edu/factsheets/boricgen.html ; https://www.aspcapro.org/sites/default/files/zm-vetm0306_142-148.pdf
2. **The graphic says:** Diatomaceous earth: Food-grade only.
   **The sources say:** NPIC does not say food-grade only. It describes over 150 registered pesticide products, some usable directly on dogs and cats, describes food-grade products as purified ones used in feed and in clarifying wine and beer, and says to always follow label instructions. The respiratory-irritant note and applying with pets out are supported.
   Source: https://npic.orst.edu/factsheets/degen.html
3. **The graphic says:** Citronella oil: Dilute heavily.
   **The sources say:** No source checked publishes a dilution for use on or around pets. NPIC calls oil of citronella mildly irritating to skin and eyes and has nothing on dogs or cats; ASPCA says to keep citronella candles, insect coils and tiki torch oil out of reach (stomach irritation, possible central nervous system depression, aspiration pneumonia if inhaled). The drooling and pawing-at-face signs are supported by a Pet Poison Helpline-authored veterinary article.
   Source: https://npic.orst.edu/factsheets/citronellagen.html ; https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/general-pet-care/fourth-july-safety-tips
4. **The graphic says:** Cedarwood oil: Cats SAFER, Dogs SAFER. Among the gentlest options.
   **The sources say:** No veterinary source checked rates cedarwood oil as gentler. Merck lists cedar among essential oils that can cause seizures (with birch, eucalyptus, hyssop, pennyroyal, sage, wintergreen, wormwood); Pet Poison Helpline lists cedar among common essential oils that can cause poisoning. Neither says whether it means cedarwood oil, cedar leaf oil or both.
   Source: https://www.merckvetmanual.com/toxicology/toxicoses-from-household-hazards/toxicoses-from-essential-oils-in-animals ; https://www.petpoisonhelpline.com/uncategorized/essential-oils/
5. **The graphic says:** White vinegar: Cats SAFE, Dogs SAFE. Food-safe.
   **The sources say:** ASPCA: vinegar is acidic and can cause stomach upset and irritation; it is not generally an issue if it is diluted, rinsed and allowed to dry. No source calls it safe.
   Source: https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/poisonous-household-products
6. **The graphic says:** Snap traps: Place inside walls/attic.
   **The sources say:** University of California: set traps close to walls, behind objects and in dark corners where there is evidence of mouse activity, no more than about 10 feet apart, where the risk of children, pets or other nontarget animals encountering the trap is low. The guide does not mention inside walls or attics, and a trap cannot be set or checked inside a finished wall.
   Source: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/PMG/PESTNOTES/pn7483.html
7. **The graphic says:** Ultrasonic repellers: Cats SAFE, Dogs SAFE.
   **The sources say:** None of the veterinary or poison-control sources checked assesses ultrasonic devices for cats, dogs or pet rodents, so a safe rating has no source behind it. (This difference was missing from the writer's callout; added.)
   Source: https://www.merckvetmanual.com/toxicology/toxicoses-from-household-hazards/toxicoses-from-essential-oils-in-animals
8. **The graphic says:** Ultrasonic repellers: Effectiveness varies, it's a supplement.
   **The sources say:** University of California: ultrasonic sound is directional, does not penetrate behind objects and loses intensity quickly with distance; there is little evidence that sound, magnetic or vibrational devices of any kind will drive established mice or rats from buildings or provide any prevention or control.
   Source: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/PMG/PESTNOTES/pn7483.html
