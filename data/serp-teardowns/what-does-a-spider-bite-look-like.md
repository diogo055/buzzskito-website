## 5. What a US reader needs that our Canadian page omits

**a) US species set.** Wolf, hobo, yellow sac, jumping, tarantula. A US searcher in the Pacific Northwest is looking for *hobo*; in the South, *wolf* and *banana*. Our page's implicit species universe is "widow, recluse, everything else" — which reads as complete in Ontario and incomplete in Oregon.

**b) Exact US ranges, stated as US ranges.** Our page currently says brown recluse "occupies a limited range in the central and southern parts of North America" — deliberately continent-neutral. To a reader in Missouri, Arkansas, Kansas or Tennessee that phrasing reads as *somewhere else*, when in fact they are **inside** the endemic range. CDC's own wording is quotable and unambiguous: black widows are found "throughout North America, but are most common in the southern and western United States"; brown recluse "in the midwestern and southern United States." Naming the recluse states outright is the single highest-value US addition — it flips the page from hedging to answering.

**c) US emergency and poison-control routing.** The page says *"your local poison control service"* and *"call your local emergency number."* Correct for Canada, useless as an instruction to an American in the moment. US readers need **Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222** (national, free, 24/7) and **911**, named explicitly. Purely additive — the Canadian phrasing stays and the US routing sits alongside it.

**d) US seasonality — an open gap in the whole SERP.** Not one top-10 result gives a seasonal answer. US spider-bite encounters cluster in **late summer through fall (roughly August–October)** when mature males wander indoors seeking mates, running effectively year-round in the Gulf and Southwest and compressing to summer in the northern tier. Nobody owns this. It is also natively on-brand for a seasonal pest business.

**e) US institutional sources.** We cite CDC and Health Canada. Add, by name, **Mayo Clinic**, **CDC NIOSH's venomous spiders page**, and **state land-grant university extension services** — the US-native answer to "who identifies my spider," and the correct destination for the specimen our "should I catch the spider?" FAQ tells people to keep. Extension entomology labs are a US-specific institution with no Canadian equivalent our page could have thought to mention.

**f) US OTC/retail context.** Our home-care list is generic ("an over-the-counter pain reliever or oral antihistamine"). Safe, but a US reader gets no orientation. Any addition here must stay non-Amazon-price, non-rating, `search=`-only per the monetisation rules.

## 6. Where `app/blog/what-does-a-spider-bite-look-like/page.tsx` falls short

**What is already strong and must not be touched.** The title tag, meta description, H1, slug and Quick Answer capsule are all well-formed and on-intent. The 4-column *spider / mosquito / bed bug / tick* comparison table is better than Terminix's bullet version and is the page's best asset. The "most suspected spider bites are something else / >80% are MRSA" angle is genuinely differentiated — **Harvard, GoodRx and Cleveland Clinic all under-play it, and PAA #3 asks for it directly.** The medical-disclaimer paragraph already exists and satisfies the health-adjacent requirement. CDC and Health Canada are already cited inline by name. `SpecialistDisclosure`, the internal links, and the `/pest-product-guides` CTA all stay.

**Specific shortfalls, all fixable additively:**

1. **Zero images or visual assets.** Against a SERP where the AIO's three citations all carry thumbnails, GoodRx and Prevention are photo-led, and `spider bite pictures` is 28,000/mo at KD 7. Biggest single gap.
2. **Only 2 of ~7 species.** No wolf (44,000/mo), hobo (7,200), yellow sac (7,300), jumping, or tarantula. Needs an additive "Other spiders that bite in the US" H2 — this is where US ranges live too.
3. **No PAA-matched headings.** None of the four verbatim PAA questions appears as an H2. *"Would you feel it if you got bit by a spider?"* is especially exposed — sensation exists only as one table cell, with no heading and no FAQ entry. *"Will spider bite go away on its own?"* is answered in substance under the healing H2 but never in Google's words.
4. **Triage is prose, not tiers.** The emergency / same-day lists are medically sound but lack the labelled, scannable tier structure the DR 7 page wins with.
5. **No prevention section.** Harvard has one; Cleveland Clinic has one; we have none. Also the natural, compliant bridge to service and product CTAs.
6. **No seasonality at all** — and nobody in the top 10 has it either. Free territory.
7. **US emergency routing missing.** "Your local emergency number" and "your local poison control service" need **911** and **1-800-222-1222** added beside them.
8. **Recluse range hedged into uselessness for a US reader.** "Central and southern parts of North America" should be *supplemented* (not replaced) with the CDC's "midwestern and southern United States" plus the named states.
9. **No "how common are spider bites?" / risk-factors / complications blocks** that Cleveland Clinic uses to out-cover everyone.
10. **Child-specific guidance is one clause.** Organic #1 is an entirely paediatric page. `when to worry about a spider bite on a child` = 450/mo, KD 5.
11. **No prognosis / "what to expect" block** as a discrete, liftable unit — currently folded into the healing narrative.
