## 4. What the top 3 pages have that we do not

### #2 Insect Shield — *No-See-Um Bites vs. Bed Bug Bites: How to Identify & Prevent Them*
The entire page is a **bed bug comparison**. Its distinguishing criteria: outdoor/dawn-dusk vs indoor/night; random clusters vs lines and zigzags ("breakfast, lunch, dinner"); immediate irritation vs delayed onset; permethrin-treated clothing as the prevention answer.

The same comparison is syndicated across the SERP's long tail — Bob Vila and Yahoo Lifestyle both run "No-See-Um Bites vs. Bed Bug Bites: 6 Ways to ID the Bug Behind the Sting," Best Bee Brothers (DR 27, #18) runs its own, plus lajaunies.com and pestsource.com. Bob Vila's version adds: bed bugs leave **dark red/black stains on bedsheets**, no-see-ums leave none; bed bugs feed every 4–7 days so bite counts accumulate faster; bed bugs need professional treatment, no-see-ums respond to repellent.

**We have no bed bug content whatsoever.** Our comparison table runs no-see-um vs mosquito vs flea vs chigger. Bed bug is the single most commercially and emotionally loaded look-alike — it is the one people panic about — and it is the axis the #1 organic result and the AI Overview are both built on.

### #4 UF/IFAS Featured Creatures — *Biting Midges, No-See-Ums Culicoides spp.*
Deep entomology, revised September 2025. Contains:
- **~170 Culicoides species in the USA**, ~50 in Florida alone, ~1,350 worldwide; plus the other biting genera *Leptoconops* and *Forcipomyia* (*Lasiohelea*).
- **Named species mapped to habitat:** *C. furens* (coastal), *C. mississippiensis*, *C. hollensis*, *C. barbosai*, *C. jamaicensis* (marsh/mangrove), *C. insignis* (wet pasture), *C. debilipalpis*, *C. guttipennis*, *C. paraensis* (wet treeholes), *C. sonorensis* and *C. variipennis* (pond/puddle edges, confirmed US disease vectors), *C. mohave* (California).
- **Dispersal distances:** many species fly ~2 km (1.2 mi); *C. variipennis* up to 4 km (2.5 mi); *C. mohave* up to 6 km (3.7 mi).
- **Full life cycle with numbers:** eggs ~0.25 mm, hatch 2–7 days; four larval instars, 2–14 days but up to a year in poor conditions; pupa 1.5 days+; *C. variipennis* egg-to-adult in 16 days.
- **Adult size 1.0–2.0 mm**, and the explicit statement that **regular window screens do not exclude them**.
- **Activity:** most species bite at dusk, night or morning; cold weather and coastal settings push biting into daytime; *C. tissoti* bites aggressively by day.
- **Disease:** Oropouche virus (vector *C. paraensis*), *Mansonella* filarial worms, and the veterinary set — bluetongue (BTV), epizootic hemorrhagic disease (EHDV), African horse sickness, Schmallenberg, and "sweet itch" in horses.
- **Repellent evidence:** DEET as the mainstay; lemon eucalyptus tested up to 100% repellency against *C. obsoletus*, outperforming DEET's 75%.

**We name zero species**, give no dispersal distance, no life-cycle numbers, and only a vague "some tropical *Culicoides* species" aside.

## 5. What a US reader needs that a Canadian page omits

**US species and ranges.** A reader in Florida, the Gulf Coast, or coastal Carolina wants to know *which* midge is biting them. *C. furens* and *C. barbosai* are the coastal salt-marsh biters; *C. sonorensis* and *C. variipennis* are the pond-edge species and the confirmed US disease vectors in livestock; *C. mohave* is the California desert species. ~170 species nationally.

**US seasonality by region — we have none.** Florida and the Gulf Coast run essentially year-round with spring and fall peaks; the northern tier runs a compressed May–September like Ontario; the desert Southwest peaks around monsoon moisture. Our page's only implicit season is the Ontario one.

**US agencies.** We cite CDC and Health Canada / PHAC. Missing entirely:
- **EPA** — this is the big one. In the US, skin repellents are **EPA-registered**, and the EPA repellent search tool is the standard citation. We cite Health Canada/PMRA-style framing only. A US reader looking for authority on DEET/picaridin expects EPA.
- **State Cooperative Extension services** — three of the top 15 results are extension pages (UF/IFAS #4, University of Arizona #10, UC ANR #15). Extension is the trusted US entity class for "what is biting me" and we reference none of them.
- **CDC's Oropouche pages** specifically (see below).

**The Oropouche gap — the sharpest currency miss on the page.** Two of the top ten results are Oropouche virus pages: **CDC at #6** ("Oropouche Puts Biting Midges Under the Microscope") and **New York State Department of Health at #9**. Google has clearly bound the biting-midge entity to Oropouche in the wake of the 2024–2025 outbreak in the Americas.

Our page currently states that North American midges "are not significant carriers of human disease" and that CDC/PHAC guidance "focuses far more on mosquitoes and ticks." That framing is defensible for *locally acquired* US infection, but it reads as stale against a SERP where the CDC's own midge story is about a virus. **We never use the word Oropouche.** Any addition here must be precise: US cases have been **travel-associated** (returning travellers, chiefly from Cuba and Brazil), the principal vector is *Culicoides paraensis*, and there is no established local transmission in the continental US — cite CDC by name and do not imply domestic risk.

**US retail context.** Fine screening is bought in the US as "20x20 no-see-um mesh" from big-box stores; our mesh table already carries threads/inch and inch measurements, which is correct and US-legible. Permethrin clothing treatment is heavily US-marketed (Insect Shield is literally a permethrin apparel brand and it holds organic #2). Our permethrin mention links out to a Canada-titled clothing guide.

**Naming variants.** US coastal readers say **sand fly, sand flea, punkie, punky, five-o'clock fly**. callwaynes ranks at #14 with "sand flea bites" (17,000 vol) as its top keyword. Our page uses only "no-see-um" and "biting midge" and never disambiguates these synonyms — including the genuinely important correction that a "sand flea" is a different animal from a biting midge.
