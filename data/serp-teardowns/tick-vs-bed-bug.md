## 5. WHAT A US READER NEEDS THAT OUR CANADIAN PAGE OMITS

This is the highest-leverage section. Our page is Ontario-locked in several load-bearing places, and in one case actively sends US readers to a dead end.

### (a) The identification CTA is Canada-only — a genuine dead end
Step 4 of "The 10-Second Check" reads:

> *"Photograph it from above and submit to eTick.ca for free tick identification within 1–3 days."*

**eTick is a Canadian platform.** It is our only identification pathway, and 15.2% of our blog readers — a share that is climbing — cannot use it.

**ADD (do not replace):** **TickSpotters**, the free photo-ID service from the **University of Rhode Island's TickEncounter** program. It returns a species ID *and* a personalised risk assessment in under 24 hours, is free, is open to anyone in the US, and has handled 60,000+ submissions (it bills itself as America's largest crowdsourced tick survey). This is the single most concrete US fix on the page.

### (b) Species — we name one; the US has five common human-biters
Our page names only the **blacklegged / deer tick**, because that is what Ontario has. Our entire size chart and the table's "Unfed size 3–5 mm" row are calibrated to that one species. Per CDC:

| Species | US range | Key diseases |
|---|---|---|
| Blacklegged tick (*Ixodes scapularis*) | Widely distributed across the **eastern US**; Northeast & Midwest | Lyme, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan |
| **Western blacklegged tick** (*Ixodes pacificus*) | **Pacific Coast, especially northern California** | Lyme, anaplasmosis |
| **Lone star tick** (*Amblyomma americanum*) | **Central Texas and Oklahoma eastward across the southern states**; also Northeast, South, Midwest | Ehrlichiosis, STARI, tularemia, **alpha-gal syndrome** |
| **American dog tick** (*Dermacentor variabilis*) | **Widely distributed east of the Rocky Mountains** (*D. similis* west of the Rockies) | **Rocky Mountain spotted fever**, tularemia |
| **Gulf Coast tick** (*Amblyomma maculatum*) | Primarily the **Southeast**, with focal populations in the Northeast, Midwest and Southwest | Rickettsiosis |

An engorged American dog tick or a lone star adult looks nothing like the 3 mm blacklegged nymph our diagram is built around — which is precisely why a US reader mistakes one for a bed bug.

### (c) Diseases — our list is the Ontario set
The table's **"Disease risk"** row reads *"Lyme, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan"*. Accurate for Ontario; **incomplete for the US**. Missing and high-volume in the US:

- **Rocky Mountain spotted fever** — the most severe rickettsiosis in the US, rapidly progressive and potentially fatal within days without early doxycycline. **Five states — North Carolina, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Tennessee and Missouri — account for over 60% of cases** (CDC). Carried by *Dermacentor*, not *Ixodes*, so it is invisible to an Ontario-framed page.
- **Alpha-gal syndrome** — red-meat allergy triggered by lone star tick bites. **CDC identified more than 110,000 suspected cases between 2010 and 2022** and estimates **up to 450,000 people in the US may be affected**. There is no Canadian analogue at this scale. It is a large and growing US search topic and we say nothing about it.
- **Ehrlichiosis** and **STARI** (lone star tick) — both named by Orkin, absent from us.
- **Tularemia.**

### (d) Scale — we give no US numbers
**CDC estimates approximately 476,000 people are diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease each year in the United States** (derived from commercial insurance claims 2010–2018; CDC notes the figure likely includes patients treated on clinical suspicion who do not have Lyme — state that caveat). A single quotable statistic of this kind is exactly what AI Overviews lift, and we already hold the citation slot.

### (e) Seasonality — ours is a single Ontario season; the US has four
Our page implies one season and states *"In Ontario, a blacklegged tick generally needs to be attached for 24 or more hours to transmit Lyme."* US seasonality is regional and in one region **inverted**:

- **CDC treats April–September as the peak exposure window**, but ticks are active **year-round in warmer climates**.
- **Northeast:** blacklegged nymph activity peaks **May–June**.
- **Upper Midwest** (WI, MN, MI): nymphs **May–July**; adults **October–early December** and again **March–May**.
- **Southeast / deep South:** lone star ticks active **nearly year-round**; mild winters mean activity through the winter months.
- **Pacific Coast:** the **western blacklegged tick is active from late fall through early spring** — the wet season, and the **exact inverse of the Northeast pattern**.

A reader in California reading "tick season is May to September" gets the wrong answer. This also matters commercially: it is the counter-seasonal angle the affiliate strategy depends on.

### (f) Agencies — we cite none, anywhere
The page currently makes unsourced medical claims: the 24-hour Lyme transmission window, and the bull's-eye rash. **No agency is named anywhere in the file.** This breaks our own medical-accuracy standard and forfeits an E-E-A-T signal that MedicineNet (CDC + EPA) uses. Every medical claim needs **CDC / Mayo Clinic / Health Canada / Public Health Ontario** named inline — and naming *both* CDC and Health Canada in the same sentence is itself the dual-market signal we want.

### (g) Prevention and repellents — an entire missing section, with a US regulatory split
No prevention content at all. US-specific additions:
- **EPA's insect repellent search tool** for registered products — DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus / PMD, 2-undecanone.
- **CDC: clothing and gear treated with 0.5% permethrin provides a higher level of protection from ticks than skin-applied repellents.**
- **Regulatory parity.** Our bed bug paragraph correctly insists on a **PCP registration number** for Canadian buyers ("buy Canadian-registered or don't buy"). That paragraph is right and must stay verbatim — but a US reader needs the parallel: an **EPA registration number**, and EPA's bed bug product search. One sentence added beside it serves both markets.

### (h) Bed bugs — the US federal framing we are missing
**EPA and CDC issued a joint statement identifying bed bugs as a public health pest**, and EPA lists them among pests of significant public health importance. Our page says "bed bugs transmit no known disease" — true, but the CDC/EPA framing (a public health pest *despite* not transmitting disease) is a sharper, better-sourced answer to PAA #2, "What's worse, bed bugs or ticks?" than anything in the top 10.

### (i) Internal links — all six exits are Canada-scoped
"Related Reading" offers 6 links, including `what-ticks-look-like-ontario`, plus 5 Canadian bed-bug links in the treatment section (`konk-bed-bug-killer-review-canada`, `harris-bed-bug-killer-review-canada`, `bed-bug-control-canada-hub`, `best-bed-bug-detector-canada`, `best-bed-bug-trap-canada`). **Every single one must stay** — they are load-bearing for the Canadian half. But a US reader has nowhere to go, which caps dwell time and kills any onward affiliate path.
