### #2 CDC — "How to Clean Up After Rodents" (DR 94)
The authoritative cleanup protocol, verbatim:
- "Put on rubber or plastic gloves."
- "Spray urine and droppings with bleach solution or an EPA-registered disinfectant until
  very wet. Let it soak for **5 minutes** or according to instructions on the disinfectant label."
- "Use paper towels to wipe up…" → "Throw the paper towels in a covered garbage can…"
- "Mop or sponge the area with a disinfectant. Clean all hard surfaces including floors,
  countertops, cabinets, and drawers."
- "Wash gloved hands with soap and water or a disinfectant **before** removing gloves."
- Bleach ratio: "**1.5 cups of household bleach in 1 gallon of water** (or 1 part bleach to
  9 parts water)."
- "**Don't vacuum or sweep** rodent urine, droppings, or nesting materials. This can cause
  tiny droplets containing viruses to get into the air."

**What it has that we do not:** the **US volumetric measurement** (1.5 cups per gallon) and
the phrase "**EPA-registered disinfectant**," which is the term of art a US reader will look
for on a product label. Our protocol gives only the 1:9 ratio.

> **Our cleanup protocol is otherwise a match for CDC, step for step, including the 5-minute
> soak, the paper-towel wipe, the double-bag, the gloves-off-last order, and the do-not-vacuum

> That is a free citation being left on the floor on a SERP where CDC is the #2 result and an

## 5. What a US reader needs that our Canadian page omits

### 5a. US species and ranges — we name the wrong mouse for most of the country

Our page tells the reader hantavirus is "associated with the deer mouse" and stops there.
Per CDC, **four** North American rodents carry HPS-causing hantaviruses, and their ranges
determine which one a US reader is actually looking at:

| Species | Range (CDC) |
|---|---|
| **Deer mouse** | "found throughout North America" — the primary carrier, Sin Nombre virus |
| **White-footed mouse** | the Northeast; also New England, mid-Atlantic, southern states, Midwest, western states |
| **Rice rat** | "from the southeastern United States to Central America" |
| **Cotton rat** | "from the southeastern United States to South America" |

A reader in Florida or Georgia is in cotton-rat and rice-rat country. Our page has no line
for them at all.

### 5b. US seasonality and regions — the geography of risk is inverted from ours

- **94% of all reported US hantavirus cases occur west of the Mississippi River** (CDC).
- Highest-case states: **New Mexico, Colorado, Arizona, California, Washington**.
- **10 states have reported no case since 1993**: Alaska, Hawaii, Missouri, Mississippi,
  Alabama, Georgia, South Carolina, Kentucky, Ohio, New Jersey.
- Our page says risk is "concentrated in the western provinces." The US analogue —
  the arid Southwest and the Four Corners region — is a completely different map and is
  where a large share of US search volume sits.

### 5c. US agencies to cite by name

| Agency | What to cite it for | We currently cite |
|---|---|---|
| **CDC** | Cleanup protocol; hantavirus case data; carrier species | ❌ "public-health agencies" |
| **EPA** | "EPA-registered disinfectant"; consumer rodenticide restrictions | ❌ nothing |
| **State Cooperative Extension** (e.g. Nebraska G1105) | Mouse biology, pellet counts, ¼-inch exclusion standard | ❌ nothing |
| **Mayo Clinic** | HPS symptom progression | ❌ nothing |

**Verified CDC hantavirus figures available to cite** (use precisely — these are two
different denominators and must not be conflated):
- **890 cases** of hantavirus disease reported in the US since surveillance began in **1993**,
  as of the end of **2023** (859 HPS + 31 non-pulmonary).
- **35%** of confirmed infections resulted in death *(CDC, Reported Cases page)*.
- **38%** of people who develop respiratory symptoms may die *(CDC, About Hantavirus page)*.
- Symptoms begin **1 to 8 weeks** after contact; respiratory symptoms appear **4 to 10 days**
  after the initial phase.
- 890 cases over 30 years across a population of ~330M is genuinely rare — the page's
  existing "don't over-frighten the reader" framing is correct and should be kept, just
  re-anchored to US numbers.

### 5d. The lizard/gecko gap — a PAA question that is purely US geography

Google is asking **"What is the difference between mouse droppings and lizard droppings?"**
because house geckos and anoles are common indoors across **Florida, the Gulf Coast, Texas,
the Southwest and Hawaii**. There is no Canadian equivalent, which is exactly why our page
has no row for it. The differentiators are clean and table-ready:

- **A chalky white cap on one end** — uric acid from the cloaca, since reptiles excrete urine
  and faeces together. Rodent pellets never have this.
- **Shape:** cylindrical, one tapered end, one rounded end with the white tip. Mouse pellets
  taper at **both** ends.
- **Contents:** visible insect fragments — beetle shell, ant heads — because lizards eat
  insects, not seeds or food scraps.
- **Location:** vertical and elevated surfaces — screens, window ledges, patio/lanai tracks,
  pool cages — not baseboards and drawers.

Adding one row to the existing ID table plus one FAQ closes a PAA slot no competitor in the
top 10 owns well.

### 5e. Units — the mechanical fix

The page is metric-only throughout: 3–6 mm, 12–19 mm, 8–10 mm, ~8 mm, 6 mm gaps,
"a few metres," "quarter-inch hardware cloth" (the one imperial survivor).
**Every US competitor leads with inches**, and the AI Overview's extracted answer is in
inches. Dual units (metric first, imperial in parentheses) satisfies both markets in one URL
without touching a single Canadian number:
- 3–6 mm → **(⅛–¼ inch)**
- 12–19 mm → **(½–¾ inch)**
- 6 mm gap → **(¼ inch — the US exclusion standard)**
- "a few metres" home range → **(within about 50 feet)**

### The one structural trap to avoid

**Item 3 must be solved additively.** The prime directive forbids rewriting or reordering, and
the Canadian health section is genuinely good content that a Canadian service-intent page
needs. Do **not** edit "The Health Picture in Canada, Told Accurately."

**Add a sibling H2 beside it** — e.g. *"The Health Picture in the United States"* — carrying
the CDC numbers from §5c, the four carrier species from §5a, and the west-of-the-Mississippi
geography from §5b. One URL then answers the question for both readers, which is exactly the
stated goal. The same pattern applies to items 5, 12 and 13: add the US paragraph next to the
Canadian one, never in place of it.

  and all 10 internal links are load-bearing — leave them.
