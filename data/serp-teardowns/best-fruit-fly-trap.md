## 5. What a US reader needs that our Canadian page omits

### Species

- We name only **Drosophila melanogaster**. Correct, but incomplete for the US.
- **Spotted wing drosophila (*Drosophila suzukii*)** — invasive, first detected in the US in **2008** (California), in Hawaii since the 1980s, spread through the West Coast in 2009, reached Florida, Utah, the Carolinas, Wisconsin and Michigan in 2010, and by **2016 was in every state except Nevada and Arizona**. ~3 mm, closely resembles *D. melanogaster*. **Males have a dark spot on the leading edge of each wing near the tip; females are told apart only by a saw-like ovipositor with two rows of dark teeth.** Behaviourally distinct: unlike vinegar flies that only exploit overripe and rotten fruit, SWD females lay eggs in **immature and ripe** fruit — which is why a US reader with a berry patch or a backyard fruit tree has a different problem than a US reader with a fruit bowl, and why vinegar-lure kitchen traps are not the answer for it.
  Sources to cite by name: **UC IPM (Pest Note 74158), University of Minnesota Extension, Penn State Extension, Michigan State University, Cornell IPM, Ohio State (Ohioline ENT-0086), WSU Tree Fruit, USDA National Invasive Species Information Center.**

### Seasonality and region

- Our key-facts table says *"Peak season in Canada: Late summer to Thanksgiving."* **Thanksgiving is October in Canada and November in the US** — this row silently misinforms a US reader by roughly a month.
- **University of Kentucky Entomology (EF-621):** fruit flies "can be a problem year round, but are especially common during late summer/fall." Also from EF-621: ~**500 eggs** per female, full egg-to-adult cycle **in about a week**, and a single rotting potato or onion forgotten at the back of a closet, or juice spillage under a refrigerator, can breed thousands of fruit flies.
- **Development effectively stops below ~53°F (~12°C)** — the mechanism that explains why the season ends, and why it barely ends at all in the Deep South.
- **Regional split we have nothing on:** the Southeast sees flies earlier and for longer; the Upper Midwest gets a shorter, sharper season; warm southern regions see low-level activity most of the year. A US reader in Phoenix, Tampa and Minneapolis are three different readers. Our page addresses one Ontario kitchen.

### Agencies and regulatory frame

- Our "Why Traps and Not Sprays in Canada (the PMRA Line)" section is genuinely useful and **must stay** — but it is Canada-only, and it currently uses US readers as the cautionary example ("If you research fruit fly control on US websites, you will meet a wall of aerosol recommendations"). A US reader arrives, is told their own market's products are the problem, and gets no US answer.
- **The exact US mirror exists and is strong.** Under **FIFRA**, a *device* is "any instrument or contrivance (other than a firearm) that is intended for trapping, destroying, repelling, or mitigating any pest," and per **EPA's Pesticide Registration Manual Chapter 13**, devices are not subject to the registration requirements that apply to pesticides under FIFRA section 3. A drowning liquid trap, a glue board and a UV unit are devices in the US for the same reason they are devices in Canada. Separately, **FIFRA section 25(b)** exempts minimum-risk pesticides from federal registration — the category several "natural" fruit fly products sit in.
- **University extension services are the US authority layer** and we cite none. UKY, UMD, UMN, UC IPM, Penn State, MSU and Cornell IPM are the sources US readers and Google both trust for household entomology.
- No **CDC** citation. Any health framing (fruit flies as mechanical contaminants on food-prep surfaces) must be sourced to CDC or a named extension service — never invented.
