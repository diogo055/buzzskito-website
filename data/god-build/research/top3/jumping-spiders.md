## The three, taken apart
1. **Wikipedia, "Jumping spider"** (~3,000 words of body). Description, vision, jumping, hunting, diet,
   courtship, taxonomy, habitat, mimicry, fossils. 15-20 photos, nearly all tropical or Old World species;
   none on a house wall or window, no scale. No table. Gives size as 1-25 mm worldwide. Nothing on bites,
   nothing on which species a North American reader meets, nothing on babies or what to do indoors.
2. **UT Austin Biodiversity Center, "Backyard Biodiversity: Jumping Spiders"** (Aug 2020, ~830 words plus
   sources). No subheads. One photo (a Habronattus male) and a Texas species poster. Eyes, hydraulic jump,
   hearing, ant mimics, courtship. No sizes, no bite section, no ID steps. Says jumpers do not build webs,
   then describes Marpissa web tents (silk retreats, not catching webs: CSU and MDC).
3. **PestWorld (NPMA), "Jumping Spiders"** (~1,200 words). Fact box (1/8-3/4 inch, black with pale marks),
   about 20 question headings (two repeated), 3 photos. Bite treatment suggests aspirin or acetaminophen
   and antihistamines with no clinical source; no sources cited at all; only the zebra spider is named; no
   look-alikes; babies covered only as egg counts. Ends with a pro-finder.

## Questions a searcher needs (our page answers each)
- What does a jumping spider look like / how do I know it is one (eyes, jerky hops, no web)?
- Which one is this: bold, Johnson's, zebra? How big do they get?
- Is it a jumping spider or a wolf spider? A black widow (black with red)? Another black spider?
- Do jumping spiders bite, are they poisonous, what happens if one bites, when to get care?
- Why is it on my windowsill or wall; do they infest homes; are they active at night?
- What do they eat, how far do they jump, why does it look at me?
- What does a baby jumping spider look like; can a baby one bite? Egg sacs and silk retreats.
- What should I do with one in the house?

## Things our page does that none of the three do
1. **A sourced quick-ID table against the look-alikes** (bold, Johnson's and zebra jumpers, wolf spider,
   black widow, false black widow, parson spider) with sizes in inches, the tell, web or no web, and bite
   at each source's strength.
2. **The eye test that separates a jumper from a wolf spider** (MDC: wolf spiders have a row of four small
   eyes under the big pair; UC IPM's three-row jumper pattern), with a face-on wolf spider photo, plus
   look-alike cards linking our wolf spider, black widow, false widow, black spider and house spider pages.
3. **Real, credited photos of the North American species people meet** (bold jumper on a leaf, Johnson's
   jumper on a grass blade), not tropical species or stock images.
4. **The bite answer with sources**: extension pages (CSU, MDC, WSU, Kentucky, Penn State) at their own
   strength, CDC's list of US venomous spiders (widow, recluse), Poison Control's care steps and number,
   and UC IPM's warning that many "spider bites" are something else. No medicine doses.
5. **Babies**: UF says spiderlings look like small adults and start out dark; egg sacs and retreats from
   UF, Kentucky, MDC and CSU.

## What they get wrong or leave out (our page states it from the source)
- PestWorld's bite care adds medicines with no source; we give Poison Control's steps only.
- UT Austin's "do not build webs" vs "web tents": jumpers spin no catching web but do make silk retreats
  and a safety line (MDC bold jumper, CSU).
- Clemson (a source, not a top-3 page) says the eyes sit "in a single row"; CSU, UC IPM and UF give three
  (or four) rows, so the page uses the rows. Clemson's 1/10-1/4 inch is also far below MDC's bold jumper
  (females to 3/4 inch): the page takes species sizes from species pages and CSU's 4-12 mm overall.
