## The three, taken apart
- **Scotts:** about 750 words; two images (a millipede on the ground, a coiled one on white; no scale, no credit);
  no table, FAQ, sources, author or date. Says 1 to 2 inches and "80-400" legs; hiding places (a list with
  duplicates); decomposers; "wait them out", sweep, vacuum or pick up; then sells a perimeter insecticide. No
  centipede comparison, no why-after-rain, one line on the defensive fluid.
- **UC IPM:** about 1,300 words; 5 photos, only one showing a millipede (beside a centipede); Table 1 centipedes vs
  millipedes; peer reviewed. Strong management: rarely needs control, sweep up, seal, dry crawl spaces, remove
  rotting wood and leaves at the foundation, invasions over within a few days, pesticides rarely justified.
  Mentions wireworms as a look-alike. California species only. Gets the leg count wrong (below).
- **Orkin:** about 1,000 words; two illustrations, no photos; size in centimeters (2.5-4 cm); no sources. Where
  they come from, are they dangerous, signs, migration (fall, after flooding), diet, life cycle, then a free quote.
  Calls millipedes harmless in one place and mentions a "toxic fluid" in another without explaining either.

## What our page does that none of the three do
1. **Real, credited photos and a quick-ID table that sorts the millipede from what it gets confused with:**
   greenhouse millipede (a common invader), American giant millipede (captioned as an outdoor species), house
   centipede on a wall; table rows for household millipede, American giant millipede, house centipede, pill bug and
   sowbug with sizes in inches and the tell (legs per segment, coils or rolls, speed), from Kentucky, Penn State,
   UC IPM and Kentucky's sowbug sheet; plus look-alike cards. Scotts and Orkin show no real identification photos.
2. **The "why so many" answer from five extension sources at once:** heavy rain and cooler weather (Penn State),
   hot dry weather (NC State), food running out or soil too wet or too dry (UC IPM), late summer and fall (SDSU),
   heavy spring rains (Clemson), and Kentucky's honest note that the cause of mass migrations is unclear. None of
   the three covers both the rain and the drought case. (bettertermite earns ~266 US visits a month on "why are
   there so many millipedes in my house": our h2.)
3. **Correct, linked numbers and health facts, and centipede vs millipede as its own section:** legs (30 to 300
   for most species, Kentucky; 160 on the common millipede, SDSU; the 1,306-leg record, Scientific Reports 2021);
   the defensive fluid at each source's strength (irritates skin, can burn the eyes, burn-like reaction; some
   release hydrogen cyanide but not enough to harm people); stains; cannot breed indoors (SDSU); dies within a few
   days (Penn State). The centipede comparison links both ways with /blog/house-centipedes. 10 FAQs in searchers'
   words.

## What they get wrong (our page states the sourced version)
- UC IPM: even the largest millipedes have "somewhat fewer than a hundred" legs. Kentucky ENTFACT-645: most species
  have 30 to 300; SDSU: the common millipede has 160; Scientific Reports (2021): *Eumillipes persephone* has up to
  1,306, and the previous record, *Illacme plenipes* of California, 750.
- Scotts: 1 to 2 inches. The kinds that come indoors are about 1 inch (Kentucky, Penn State, NC State; Clemson
  gives 1/2 to 3/4 inch for garden millipedes); the giant American millipede reaches about 4 inches (Kentucky).
- Orkin: maturity "in year two". UC IPM: they mature in 2 to 5 years and live several years after that.
- Scotts and Orkin never say what the fluid does. UC IPM: can irritate skin or burn the eyes; Kentucky: burn-like
  skin reactions, cyanide not enough to harm humans; Clemson: stains when crushed, can cause allergic reactions,
  wash hands; no bites to people or pets.
- Scotts makes a perimeter spray the main fix. SDSU: spraying is not recommended and perimeter sprays have limited
  effectiveness; NC State: no use trying pesticides; UC IPM: rarely justified; Kentucky: indoor pesticides rarely
  necessary.
