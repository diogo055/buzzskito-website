## The three, taken apart
- **Scotts:** about 750 words; two images (one on the ground, one coiled on white; no scale, no credit); no table,
  FAQ, sources, author or date. Says 1 to 2 inches and "80-400" legs; hiding places (a list with duplicates); "wait
  them out", sweep or vacuum; then sells a perimeter insecticide. No centipede comparison, no why-after-rain.
- **UC IPM:** about 1,300 words; 5 photos, one showing a millipede (beside a centipede); Table 1 centipedes vs
  millipedes; peer reviewed. Strong on management (rarely needs control, sweep up, seal, dry crawl spaces, clear
  rotting wood and leaves, invasions over in days, pesticides rarely justified); names wireworms as a look-alike.
  California species only. Gets the leg count wrong (below).
- **Orkin:** about 1,000 words; two illustrations, no photos; size in centimeters; no sources. Where they come from,
  are they dangerous, migration (fall, after flooding), diet, life cycle, then a free quote. Calls them harmless in
  one place and mentions a "toxic fluid" in another without explaining either.

   millipede, American giant millipede (captioned as an outdoor species), house centipede on a wall; table rows for

2. **The "why so many" answer from five extension sources:** heavy rain and cooler weather (Penn State), hot dry
   weather (NC State), food running low or living places too wet or too dry (UC IPM), spring and fall, late summer

3. **Correct, linked numbers and health facts, with centipede vs millipede as its own section:** legs (30 to 300

   not enough to harm people); stains; cannot breed indoors (SDSU); dies within days (Penn State). The comparison

## What they get wrong (our page states the sourced version)
- UC IPM: even the largest millipedes have "somewhat fewer than a hundred" legs. Kentucky: most species have 30 to
  300; SDSU: the common millipede has 160; Scientific Reports (2021): *Eumillipes persephone* has up to 1,306, and
  the previous record holder, *Illacme plenipes* of California, 750.
- Scotts: 1 to 2 inches. The kinds that come indoors are about 1 inch (Kentucky, Penn State, NC State; Clemson gives
  1/2 to 3/4 inch for garden millipedes); the giant American millipede reaches about 4 inches (Kentucky).
- Orkin: maturity "in year two". UC IPM: they mature in 2 to 5 years and live several years after that.
- Scotts and Orkin never say what the fluid does. UC IPM: can irritate skin or burn the eyes; Kentucky: burn-like
  skin reactions, cyanide not enough to harm humans; Clemson: stains, allergic reactions, wash hands, no bites.
- Scotts makes a perimeter spray the main fix. SDSU: perimeter sprays of limited effectiveness; UC IPM: rarely justified.
