## The top 3, taken apart
1. **Wikipedia, "Drugstore beetle"** (about 3,000 words). Sections on description, food, life history, genetics,
   mating, fungal symbionts, pest control, economic impact. Six images, one with a fingertip for scale, none in a
   pantry or spice jar that can be checked. No table, no FAQ, no how-to-find-the-source steps. Cigarette beetle
   comparison is two sentences (club vs serrate antennae, grooved vs smooth). Control temperatures in Celsius from
   lab papers; its own tags flag "better source needed" on the essential-oil advice.
2. **Penn State Extension, "Drugstore beetle"** (about 600 words, prepared 1988 by D. Calvin). Two Bugwood photos,
   no scale. Sizes: about 1/10 inch, larva about 2/10 inch. Control section is written for farm grain bins:
   monitoring bins every two weeks, a protective insecticide on grain stored over six months, fumigation. Nothing
   on homes, spices, bedrooms, freezing or the cigarette beetle.
3. **University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum, "Drugstore beetles"** (about 420 words, no author or date). Two
   unlabeled photos. Size in mm only (2.5-3.0 mm). Mentions adults at a night light or window. Never mentions the
   cigarette beetle. Heat and cold figures are museum figures (125°F for 2-4 hours; -20°F for two days), colder
   than a home freezer runs.

Questions at least one of them answers: what it looks like, size, antenna club and grooved wing covers, what it eats
(including drugs, books, museum specimens), larvae, life cycle, name origin, why adults are at windows, control.

## What our page does that none of the three do
- **A real drugstore vs cigarette beetle comparison**: a trait table (antennae, wing covers, outline, head, size,
  larvae, foods) and a jar checklist, with a photo of each adult and each larva. Arkansas and Penn State never name
  the cigarette beetle; Wikipedia gives it two sentences.
- **Photos where people find it, credited**: beetles among bored spice seeds in a Texas spice display, old ground
  cumin riddled with holes (Oregon), a beetle on a wall, a larva from a spaghetti box. None of the three has one.
- **Why they show up in bedrooms and on windowsills, and a room-by-room source hunt**: flight and light (Kentucky,
  Alabama, Iowa State, Virginia Tech), then the non-food sources by room (bookcases, dried flowers and potpourri,
  medicines and herbs, pet treats, rice heating pads and bean bags, rodent caches in walls and attics).
- **Home-freezer and oven numbers with their sources** (0°F for 4 days, Kentucky; 0°F for 6 days and 2 weeks for pet
  food, Texas A&M; 155°F for 20 minutes for decorations, Kentucky) and the no-spray guidance (UC IPM, UF/IFAS,
  Texas A&M), against Penn State's grain-bin insecticide advice and Arkansas's -20°F.
- **A quick-ID table and look-alike cards** for weevils, spider beetles, flour beetles, bed bugs and carpet beetles,
  and a bed bug section for people who find the beetle in a bed.

## What they get wrong or leave misleading
- Penn State's control advice (protective insecticide on stored grain, fumigation) is farm-bin advice; for homes
  UC IPM says insecticides are not recommended for any pantry pest and UF/IFAS says use them only as a last resort.
- Arkansas's freezing figure (-20°F for two days) is a museum treatment; Kentucky gives 0°F for 4 days in a freezer
  that does not defrost itself, and Texas A&M 0°F for six days, which a home freezer can do.
- A claim in the search snippets for "drugstore beetle vs cigarette beetle" (comparison pages below the top 3, not
  opened) says both adult and larval cigarette beetles feed; UF/IFAS EENY-227 says adults do not feed but will drink
  liquids, and the Bohart Museum says drugstore beetle adults apparently do not feed either. Our page says the larvae
  do the damage (UF/IFAS EENY-228) and makes no claim about adults feeding.
- "The head" as a tell: both beetles tuck the head under a hood (Virginia Tech, Texas A&M); we say so instead of
  implying the head separates them.
