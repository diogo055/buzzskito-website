# Beat the top 3: snake-identification ("snake identification", "what kind of snake is this")

Read Nov 10, 2026. Google page 1 (orchestrator's Nov 9 read): herpetology societies, New Jersey's environment
department and a snake ID app; no Wikipedia. Skipped: the app (not editorial), forums and "is this snake
venomous?" photo threads, AI identifier pages, YouTube. The search tool did not show Google's own order, so
the three below are the editorial pages named in that read plus the university key that ranks with them.

## 1. Virginia Herpetological Society, "Snake Identification Guide" (dichotomous key)
- ~1,100 words, Virginia only. A/B couplets, ~100 images (specimens, head and scale diagrams), state range
  maps. No FAQ, no sources, no first aid, no advice on meeting a snake. Updated Dec 2023.
- First split: pupils plus heat pits, then scale counts; both need a close look at the snake.
- Errors: section 28 is a dead end (option A only); caption and text disagree on scientific names (Elaphe
  vs Pantherophis); copperhead vs cottonmouth split by adult color beside a banded young cottonmouth photo.

## 2. New Jersey DEP Fish and Wildlife, "Snake Identification: Avoid Being Confused!" (PDF, 6 pp., rev. 4/19)
- ~1,600 words, the best of the three: color is not the best method, use pattern; NJ's two venomous snakes and
  where they live; copperhead hourglass vs water snake "reverse hourglass" vs milk snake blotches; patterned
  young of black rat snakes and racers; garter and hognose; tail-shaking mimics; illegal to kill or handle.
- Gaps: NJ only (22 species); legal-size PDF, poor on a phone; no first aid; no FAQ; no ranges elsewhere.

## 3. University of Kentucky, "ID Your Snake" (kysnakes.mgcafe.uky.edu/id_snake)
- ~600 words plus a filter (head shape, pupil, pit, body, pattern, color) over 33 Kentucky species. No
  sources or photo credits, no FAQ, no first aid, no "keep your distance". States "venomous snakes have
  elliptical pupils" with no caveat.

## What they get wrong (and the source that says otherwise)
- Pupils as a test (UK, VHS): Texas Parks and Wildlife says the venomous coral snake has round pupils; seeing
  pupils or pits means getting close, against CDC NIOSH (do not touch or handle) and NC Wildlife (keep a
  respectful distance).
- Head shape (UK filter): UF/IFAS UW258 says many nonvenomous snakes have triangular heads; WDFW says the
  gopher snake's head looks more triangular when agitated; SREL says the coral snake's head is small and round.
- No first aid anywhere. CDC NIOSH gives first aid and the do-not list; MedlinePlus says call 911 and gives
  Poison Help 1-800-222-1222.
- For the fact-checker: NC Wildlife's garter snake page says adults average 3 to 4 feet; SREL gives 18 to 26
  inches (up to 49) and UF/IFAS 2 to 3 feet. The page uses SREL.

## Three-plus things our page does that none of them do
1. Sorts the whole country's common yard snakes the way people describe them (stripes, blotches, solid black,
   small and plain, green, bands or rings) in a "pattern you see" sorter, with a 12-row quick-ID table of
   sourced adult lengths and the venomous status of each.
2. A range note for every snake from its agency or museum source, CDC's four venomous groups by region and a
   state count table (NJ, NC, FL, TX, WA), and the West (gopher snake vs rattlesnake, WDFW and UC IPM).
3. Agency-identified photos of adult snakes where people meet them (porch, lawn, mulch, sand, pavement), the
   failing rules of thumb named with their sources, CDC first aid with 911, look-alike cards and 10 FAQs.
