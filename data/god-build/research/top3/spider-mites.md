## The three, taken apart
1. **UC IPM, Pest Notes: Spider Mites** (Publication 7405, L.D. Godfrey, updated 12/2011). About 2,300 words with the quick tips: identification, life cycle, damage, monitoring, biological, cultural and chemical control. 5 UC IPM photos (mites, eggs, stippling), no ruler or coin. The strongest source, and our backbone. No table, no FAQ, no look-alike section beyond one line (webbing separates spider mites from aphids and thrips). The tap test is one sentence. Nothing on houseplants in winter, clover mites, spider webs, pollinators, pets or children.
2. **Wikipedia, "Spider mite"**. About 600 words of body: description, life cycle, genera, countermeasures (predatory mites, Harpin, acaricides, environmental conditions). 2 photos and a video, no damage photo, no scale. Sizes in metric only (under 1 mm). No table, no FAQ, no tap test, no step-by-step control. 15 references, one a vendor product page tagged as a dead link, and one sentence tagged "clarification needed".
3. **Ohio State CFAES, "Spider Mites and Their Control"** (HYG-2012, David J. Shetlar, Sept 2011). About 1,400 words: damage, life cycles, twospotted, spruce, five control options. 7 images (6 photos and a USDA drawing), no scale. No table, no FAQ. Has the white-paper test and a crush-streak test. Names professional miticides by brand.
- Also read: **bugsforbugs.com.au**, an Australian predatory-mite seller. About 700 words, 17 photos, no scale, "under 1 mm" the only size, no tap test, no table, no FAQ, no sources. It advises preventive releases of the predators it sells.

## What our page does that none of them do
1. **A quick-ID table under the answer**: twospotted and spruce spider mites, clover mite, aphid, thrips and predatory mite, each with a sourced size in inches and the one tell. None of the three has a table.
2. **The white-paper test as numbered steps**, with a friend-or-foe check for predatory mites (shape, legs, speed: UC IPM, UConn). Wikipedia and bugsforbugs lack the test; UC IPM gives it one sentence.
3. **Look-alikes with real photos and cards**: clover mites on windowsills (Penn State), thrips (UC IPM), aphids' cornicles, springtails, whiteflies, and a spider web against mite webbing (UMD, Colorado State). None of the three covers clover mites or spider webs.
4. **The winter-houseplant angle**: Iowa State (dry indoor air), WSU (warm, dry homes; pebble trays), Clemson (palms and English ivy) and UMD's list of commonly hit houseplants. UC IPM and Wikipedia skip it; Ohio State mentions houseplants only as a source of mites.
5. **The safety the three leave out**: no soap or oil on water-stressed plants or above 90°F, test first and wait 48 hours, the soap-sensitive houseplants that are also mite favorites (ivy, palms), no dish detergent, the label must name mites, no sprays on indoor herbs, sulfur never within 30 days of oil, NPIC's pollinator rule, children and pets.

## What they get wrong (our page states it from the source)
- Ohio State: "less than 1/50 inch (0.4 mm)". 1/50 inch is about 0.5 mm. UC IPM: adult females less than 1/20 inch; UConn: about 1/50 inch. Our page gives the sources' inches and converts nothing.
- Ohio State: summer twospotted mites are "white with two greenish spots". UConn: green to yellow with two dark spots; WSU: straw-colored, often with two dark blotches; UC IPM: females usually have a large dark blotch on each side. Our page uses those three.
- Ohio State names professional miticides (abamectin, bifenazate, hexythiazox, spiromesifen) that homeowners can buy from professional suppliers and websites. Colorado State's table lists abamectin, hexythiazox and spiromesifen for commercial use. Our page names no miticide beyond soap and oil, and points to the label (UMD: it must say it controls or suppresses mites).
- Ohio State's crush test (a green streak means a pest, yellow or orange a predator) disagrees with UMD (reddish-brown streaks). Our page leaves streak color out and uses UC IPM's shape-and-speed check.
- bugsforbugs, a seller, advises preventive predator releases. UC IPM says released predators starve or leave when there are no pest mites to eat; UMD says home gardeners need not buy predators for outdoor plants, and lab-raised ones may not suit the lower humidity indoors.
- Wikipedia cites a vendor page for a Harpin product, now a dead link, as a control.
