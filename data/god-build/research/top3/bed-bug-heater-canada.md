## Corrected (false against the primary source)
- Thermal death points (FAQ 2, physics section, Quick Answer bullet, key facts): "eggs need approximately 90 minutes at 48°C", "die
  in minutes once temperatures pass 50°C", adults "within about 20 minutes at 48°C" -> Kells & Goblirsch (2011) abstract: adults
  LTime99 94.8 min at 45°C; eggs survived 7 h at 45°C and only 71.5 min at 48°C; Health Canada: bedbugs die at 50°C; ZappBug's
  "industry standard" 120°F for one hour.
- "ZappBug Room 9" (FAQ 5, Best Overall section): ZappBug's comparison lists only Heater, Oven 2 and Room; the Room (4 heaters,
  111 x 57.5 x 80 in) is listed for couches and mattresses including king.
- Oven 2 "roughly a metre long and over half a metre in height and depth inside" (FAQ 5, body) -> ZappBug's 39.5 x 39.5 x 28 in.
- "Smallest chamber that still swallows checked luggage ... books" -> ZappBug lists luggage, bedding, shoes and books for the smaller
  Heater too; the Oven 2 adds small furniture.
- Electronics "most manufacturers rate storage well above 50°C" (FAQ 9) -> Apple lists the iPhone 16 non-operating range as -20° to
  45°C; ZappBug's Oven 2 walkthrough says to check the manufacturer's maximum and treat electronics alone for 20 min at 120°F.
- "Most domestic-class bed bug sprays carry no approved egg-kill claim because they cannot demonstrate one" (FAQ 3, FAQ 10, Legal
  Moat, key facts, bullet) -> the three Canadian labels opened (KONK 31110, Sleep Tight II 29339, Ortho MAX 33722) contain no egg
  claim at all; "most" and the reason were unverifiable.
- "Crossfire, Temprid FX, and Bedlam Plus ... not PMRA-registered for sale or use in Canada" (FAQ 11, Quick Answer bullet, body) ->
  CrossFire and Bedlam Plus have no registration and Temprid FX none by that name, but Temprid SC 32524, Temprid ReadySpray 32523
  and Bedlam Insecticide 30075 are registered, commercial class (same wording as the tier-3 do-bed-bug-sprays page).
- "Pyrethroid-resistant populations (which now dominate Canadian cities)", "widespread pyrethroid resistance in Canadian bed bug
  populations", "there is no such thing as a heat-resistant bed bug" -> unsourced for Canada; now Rutgers (2014) on pyrethroid
  resistance and "insecticide resistance does not protect a bed bug from heat". Kells & Goblirsch discuss why bed bugs can withstand
