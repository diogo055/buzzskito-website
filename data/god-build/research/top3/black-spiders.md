## The three, taken apart
- **Wikipedia:** about 2,900 words on one species; 11 images plus a range map, one of them a 1902 drawing, none with a
  scale. Sizes: females 8 to 15 mm, males 6 to 13 mm (metric first). Covers naming, vision, life cycle, range, bites.
  Bite advice (aspirin, acetaminophen) rests on WebMD; several claims unsourced. Nothing on any other black spider.
- **WSU Bug Info:** about 180 words; 2 uncredited photos of Phidippus audax; no size, no range, no sources, no date.
  Calls it the "orchard spider", black with an "irregular orange to white" spot; bite painful but not harmful.
- **Orkin:** about 1,100 words; one illustration, no photograph; no sources. Female 3/8 to 3/4 inch, male 1/4 to 1/2
  inch; "three white spots" on most; muddled range sentence; four FAQs; ends in a quote form and a sales pitch.

## What our page does that none of the three do
1. **It sorts every black spider a US reader is likely to meet, by the clue they see** (white spots, a white band, red
   on the back, red underneath, size, web or no web), not one species: bold and Johnson's jumping spiders, eastern
   parson spider, black widows (southern, northern, western, red; males and young), false black widow, wolf spiders,
   purseweb spiders, black-and-yellow garden spider. All three top pages assume the reader has a bold jumper, yet
   autocomplete shows "not fuzzy", "looks like black widow", "white stripe on back", "red on back" and "big".
2. **A quick-ID table with sourced sizes in inches and a "danger to people" column at each source's strength**
   (MDC, UC IPM, Penn State, Army Public Health Center, CDC NIOSH), and real, credited photos where people find these
   spiders: a parson spider found in a tarp in a garage, a western black widow beside her egg sacs in a corner showing
   the hourglass, a northern black widow showing the red mark on her back, a wolf spider carrying spiderlings, a
   Johnson's jumping spider on grass. Orkin has one drawing; WSU and Wikipedia credit nothing and show no scale.
3. **Safety done right:** the widow section says how to tell a widow (including the broken hourglass and back spots of
   the northern black widow, and white-striped males and young that are not medically important), never suggests
   handling one, and gives bite advice only from CDC NIOSH, Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) and UC IPM. Look-alike cards
   link the jumping spider, black widow, false widow and wolf spider pages; 10 FAQs in searchers' words.

## What they get wrong or leave out (our page states the sourced version)
- Orkin: females 3/8 to 3/4 inch; MDC: females 1/4 to 3/4 inch, males usually under 1/2 inch.
- Orkin and WSU frame the spots as "three white" or "orange to white"; MDC: white, orange or red, the central one
  largest, and CSU says jumping spider patterns vary within a species. Our page says to check shape and behavior too.
- Wikipedia's bite treatment advice (aspirin, acetaminophen) rests on WebMD; our page gives no medicines, only CDC
  NIOSH first aid and when to call Poison Control or seek care.
- WSU: "the bite is painful and can penetrate tough human skin"; MDC calls the bite harmless and CSU says most jumping
  spiders' jaws are too small to pierce skin, and a successful bite causes only mild, temporary pain.
- None warns that a shiny, smooth black spider with white or red marks may be a young or male black widow, or that the
  name "black house spider" belongs to an Australian species (Australian Museum).
