## The three, taken apart
- **Orkin** (Signs of Termite Damage; its sister page /pests/termites/damage was read too): about 300 words, three
  stock-looking photos named by file (a home, a bay window, wallpaper), no table, no FAQ, no sources, no author or
  date. Says termites cost Americans "more than $5 billion" a year; its own /damage page says $1 to $2 billion.
  Lists "a mildew- or mold-like odor" as a sign (no source). Wood rot not mentioned. Ends in a quote button.
- **Viking Pest** (What Does Termite Damage Look Like?): 1,000 to 1,200 words, a hero image and one inline photo
  (origin not stated), no table, no FAQ, no sources, no author or date. Signs: sagging floors, hollow wood, mud
  tubes ("pencil-sized"), wings, frass; where to look; prevention; bait and liquid treatments; "can you hear
  termites". Written for NJ, PA, DE and MD. Treats sagging floors and sticking doors as termite signs and never
  mentions rot or other causes. Ends in a free-estimate pitch.
- **EPA** (Termites: How to Identify and Control Them, updated Oct 8, 2026): about 1,100 words, **no photos**.
  Strong on what to do: probe wood with a screwdriver, ant vs termite swarmers, prevention list, treatment types
  and active ingredients, state licensing, read the label, "products that only claim to kill termites" do not
  protect structures. Nothing on what damage looks like, rot, look-alikes or where each termite lives.

## Questions at least one of them answers (our page answers each)
What termite damage looks like in wood; signs (mud tubes, swarmers, wings, droppings, hollow wood, paint and
wall changes); where to look; why subterranean termites do the most damage; can you hear termites (we skip: no
source we opened covers termite sounds); prevention; treatment options; what to do and who to hire.

## What our page does that none of the three do
1. **Real, credited photos of what the reader is looking at**: damaged wood in a building (NPS, public domain),
   mud tubes on an inside wall (USMC, public domain), swarmers and live workers and soldiers in wood (CC0), each
   captioned only with what its record supports. EPA has no photos; Orkin and Viking show uncredited images.
2. **A quick-ID table of the eight things that damage wood** (subterranean, Formosan, drywood and dampwood
   termites, carpenter ants, powderpost beetles, carpenter bees, wood rot): what the wood looks like, what is
   left in or under it, where it turns up. None of the three compares termite damage with anything.
3. **Termite damage vs wood rot**, which none of the three covers (it is a "people also search" term): USDA Forest
   Products Laboratory on brown rot (cube-like cracking), the "dry rot" misnomer and the 20% moisture line, and
   Mississippi State's rule that galleries holding dried mud settle it.
4. **The one test, bolded**: look inside the galleries (mud, pellets, clean walls, or no galleries at all).
5. **Old or active?** Mississippi State's 1-inch mud tube check and pinhole check; UC IPM and UF/IFAS on why
   damage or pellets alone cannot tell you.
6. **Where each termite lives**, from Texas A&M, UF/IFAS and the USDA Forest Products Laboratory; 10 FAQs from
   Google's questions; every fact linked to a government or university source; no sales pitch.

## What they get wrong or leave unsourced (our page states the sourced version)
- Damage cost: Orkin gives two different unsourced national figures ($5 billion+ and $1 to $2 billion). EPA says
  termites cause billions of dollars in structural damage a year and owners spend over two billion to treat
  them. We use EPA's wording and no other figure.
- Sagging floors and sticking doors as termite signs (Viking): Mississippi State says sagging floors, roofs or a
  broken door frame could be rot or other causes; galleries with dried mud are what make it termite damage.
- "Pencil-sized" mud tubes (Viking) and a mold-like odor (Orkin): no source we opened gives either, so we leave
  them out. Texas A&M says tubes can be thin or large with thick walls.
- Grain direction as the test: carpenter ant galleries also run with the grain (NC State; USDA FPL text), so the
  page uses what is in the galleries, not their direction.
