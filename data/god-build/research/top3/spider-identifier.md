## The three, taken apart
- **insectio.com**: photo upload plus an optional address; about 900 words on the tool page; no spider photos in the
  text, no sources, no way to confirm a result, 10 FAQs. Promises an "instant ID" for "any spider"; calls wolf spiders
  harmless with no caveat; lists the hobo spider as medically significant (UC IPM: no longer considered of probable
  medical importance; CDC's page dropped it after 2012).
- **scanabug.com**: photo upload; about 3,500 words; a web-type table and a species chart; recluse and widow sections; 8
  references, several to home pages; 16 FAQ questions with no visible answers. Unsourced accuracy claims ("cross
  referenced", "tested each quarter"); tells readers to measure leg span, then gives body lengths in its chart; nothing
  on where the reader lives beyond the recluse's absence from California.
- **insectidentification.org/spiders**: about 200 words over a list of 125 species, filtered by color only; no
  identification features, ranges, bites, sources or confirm step on the page. Lists harvestmen as spiders (MDC and UC
  Riverside: they are not) and repeats the "we swallow spiders in our sleep" claim with no study named.

## What our page does that none of the three do
1. Narrows the list from the reader's own clues (place, web, size with legs, markings, eyes and legs, behavior, region)
   and shows the sourced reason for every suggestion; "most likely" or "not clear", never a verdict.
2. Weighs where the reader lives: the recluse loses points outside its 16 established states (Penn State) and doubly in
   the North and Canada (Vetter 2005; Bennett and Vetter 2004), and says why; southern-only spiders drop out up north.
3. Says how to confirm each answer (six eyes in pairs, the hourglass underneath, the web, the egg sac) and how to get an
   expert ID (county extension office; BugGuide and iNaturalist named only as places to ask).
4. Real, credited photos of all 20 results, a 20-row comparison table with UKY's coin sizes, 10 FAQs from People also
   ask, 45 government, university, museum and peer-reviewed sources, each linked.
5. Calm safety: the safety box with every result (CDC first aid, Mayo Clinic's get-care list, Poison Help), widow,
   recluse and bite warnings, the not-spiders (harvestman, camel spider) named as such.

## Source notes
- Verified: CDC NIOSH's venomous spider page listed "black widow, brown recluse, and hobo spiders" (Internet Archive
  capture, Dec 31, 2012) and names only the black widow and brown recluse in 2016 and on the current page (Sept 28, 2026).
  Not cited on the page (UC IPM's hobo note says the same thing more usefully).
- Sources disagree, so the page gives both or neither: tarantula wandering season (Texas A&M AgriLife: May to August in
  Texas; University of Arkansas: August to November); woodlouse spider eyes (UC IPM: two groups of three; Penn State: six
  in an oval) and front color (Penn State: reddish-orange; UKY: reddish-brown; UMN: purplish-brown); parson spider color
  (UMN: brownish body, gray abdomen with a white band).
- Coin sizes are UKY's, written for Kentucky spiders; "no other common spider makes an orb web" is UKY's Kentucky
  statement and the page says so. Black widow body: UKY and UC IPM 1/2 inch, MDC 3/8 inch (the page uses UKY).
- Canada: Bennett and Vetter (Canadian Family Physician 2004): "Brown recluse spiders are not found in Canada";
  black widows from the US Army Public Health Center (northern, to southeastern Canada) and Environment and Climate Change
  Canada (western, southern BC, Alberta, Saskatchewan). No Canadian museum page on dock spiders was found; the page does
  not use the name.
