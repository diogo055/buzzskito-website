## What they answer
A list of bugs, each with a "how to tell" paragraph; lice have no wings; booklice live in damp places; fleas jump.
None says where the reader found the speck matters, none gives a check to run, none covers specks (dandruff,
hairspray, hair casts) except in passing, none says when to see a provider.

## What they get wrong (and the source that says otherwise)
- Baker: booklice "about 0.2 inches" long. Penn State Extension: 1 to 2 mm (about 1/25 to 1/12 inch); Utah State
  Extension: 1 to 4 mm.
- Wright: bed bug width "0.05 - 0.01 inches" (an impossible range) and adults "0.16 - 0.20 inches"; EPA gives adults
  as 3/16 to 1/4 inch (5 to 7 mm). Wright never says lice cannot jump; CDC: they cannot hop or fly.
- Drerup: lice "crawl slowly"; UGA Extension says they crawl rapidly, and the AAP's parent page says they move
  quickly. Drerup: fleas are "rounder"; UC IPM: fleas are flattened side to side.
- All three list dust mites, thrips, gnats and springtails as lice look-alikes with no source. None of the health
  or extension sources opened for this page (CDC, AAP, Harvard's lab, UGA, UC IPM, NC State, Arizona, Nebraska)
  names them. The one insect the AAP does name, aphids blown into the hair, appears on none of the three.

## What our page does that none of them do
1. Only look-alikes a health agency or university entomologist reports, each linked to that source: aphids (AAP
   clinical report, Harvard), booklice and barklice (Harvard's lab, Utah State), bed bugs and fleas in the bed
   (Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station poster, Harvard), bird mites (Harvard, WSU, Kentucky), specks (CDC,
   AAP, AAD), with UGA's figure that dandruff, fibers, scabs and other insects are taken for lice 40% of the time.
2. A where-did-you-find-it sorter (on the hairs, loose in the hair, after time outdoors, books, bed, clothing
   seams, the pet, near a bird nest) and the one check: six clawed legs crawling on a hair, no jumping or flying.
3. Real credited photos where people find them: a louse on a lice comb, a booklouse on a notebook page beside a pen
   tip, a winged aphid on an arm, a flea on a flea comb (CDC), tick-trefoil seeds stuck to pants beside a ruler.
4. Body lice and crab lice vs head lice (CDC), a table of "lice" that are not lice (book, bark, plant, beggar's,
   sea, pet), which look-alikes bite, how to confirm before treating (AAP: treat only living lice), when to see a
   provider, and 10 FAQs.

## Left out on purpose
Springtails, thrips, gnats and dust mites: no CDC, AAP, state health or extension source opened for this page
names them as mistaken for lice (the only springtail-and-lice claims found came from a commercial ID lab's FAQ and
a disputed 2004 study). Ticks get one line in the check list (CDC: check children in and around the hair), not an
entry.
