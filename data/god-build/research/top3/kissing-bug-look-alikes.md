## What our page does that none of the three do
1. **A quick-ID table with sourced sizes and the one tell per look-alike, plus a Chagas column at each source's
   strength:** CDC sizes for the 11 US species (0.5 to 1.3 inches), wheel bug to 1 1/2 inches (MDC, Penn State),
   masked hunter about 1/2 inch (Utah State), western conifer seed bug 3/4 inch (Penn State), leaf-footed bugs 3/4 to
   1 inch (UC IPM), boxelder bug to 1/2 inch (Penn State), brown marmorated stink bug 14 to 17 mm (EPA). CDC is cited for
   "does not carry the parasite" only where CDC names the bug (wheel bug, western corsair, leaf-footed bug); Texas A&M
   ("no insects other than kissing bugs are known to carry it") for the rest. None of the three gives a size table.
2. **The all-features test from Texas A&M in the quick answer, then ordered checks** (cone-shaped head, straight beak
   not curved and not held flat, thin legs without spines or swellings, striped edge), and real credited photos where
   people find the look-alikes: a western conifer seed bug on a computer keyboard, a milkweed assassin bug on a white
   wall, a wheel bug on ivy, a masked hunter on weathered wood, beside CDC's specimen photo of the eastern kissing bug
   with its size bar. Look-alike cards link each sibling page.
3. **What to do with a suspected kissing bug, correctly sourced and US-wide:** do not touch it bare-handed; glove or
   small plastic bag; closed container; freeze; note date, place and bites; send photos (Texas A&M); Texas residents
   with a human exposure can have it tested free by Texas DSHS, which asks for no alcohol or preservatives. Chagas facts
   from CDC (droppings into broken skin, eyes or mouth; present in the US but not regularly spread by the bugs; MMWR:
   likelihood low even when the bug is infected). FAQs in searchers' words (autocomplete).

## What they get wrong (our page states the sourced version)
- WA DOH Medium: "Chagas disease can be spread to humans through the bite of the kissing bug." CDC: it mainly spreads
  from the bugs' droppings entering broken skin, the eyes or the mouth; CSU: not transmitted when the insect bites but
  in the feces; Texas A&M: the parasite is not in the saliva or bite.
- Ohio State BYGL: the eastern kissing bug is "over 1 inch" long. CDC lists T. sanguisuga at 0.6 to 0.9 inch and Penn
  State at 0.6 to 0.8 inch (16 to 21 mm); only some southwestern species (T. gerstaeckeri, T. recurva) pass an inch.
- Ohio State BYGL: boxelder bugs 1/2 to 3/4 inch. Penn State: up to 1/2 inch (12 mm).
- WA DOH Medium calls the western conifer seed bug harmless and CSU says it "cannot bite humans"; Penn State, more
  carefully, says it does not generally bite people (one bite recorded worldwide) and is not a medical risk. Our page
  uses Penn State's wording.
- None of the three says what to do with a suspected bug; none mentions that the look-alike assassin bugs bite
  painfully when handled (MDC, Utah State, UF/IFAS), while kissing bug bites are usually painless (CSU).

## CDC's own handling advice: not found
CDC's current Chagas pages (About, How It Spreads, Triatomine Bugs in the U.S., Prevention; reviewed Feb 2026) and its
public fact sheet give no instructions for touching, containing or submitting a bug. The old CDC FAQ wording ("do not
touch or squash it ... rubbing alcohol or ... freeze") survives only in news copies and the NPS page. The page uses
Texas A&M (which CDC lists among its Chagas resources) and Texas DSHS for the handling steps, and CDC for the disease.
