## The three
- **University of Arkansas, "Drain Flies - What are they? How do I get rid of them?"** (about 950 words, no author or
  date): one adult photo borrowed from Wikipedia, no larva or drain photo, no table, no sources. Good on the cup and
  tape tests, the 3-week cleaning rule, "no insecticide is registered for drains", and "broken pipe under a slab" when
  cleaning fails. Its size line is wrong: "2-4 mm (1/16-3/16")" mixes two ranges.
- **Wikipedia, "Psychodidae"** (about 900 words of prose): taxonomy, life cycle (30 to 100 eggs laid just above the
  water line, hatching within 48 hours; adults live about 20 days), a myiasis and asthma note, six SEM images. No how-to, no test for the source, no photo of a bathroom.
  Some life-cycle claims rest on pest-company and drain-cleaning sites.
- **greendrains.com, "How to Permanently Stop Drain Flies"** (about 2,000 words): written to sell a one-way drain seal,
  called "the only permanent solution" throughout; cutaway illustrations; plausible but unsourced life-cycle numbers;
  dismisses enzyme cleaners as "rarely" working and calls drain flies a "direct health code violation", both
  unsourced. The tape-over-the-drain test is right.

## What they get wrong, and what our page says instead
- Arkansas "2-4 mm (1/16-3/16")": our sizes come from UC IPM (about 1/8 inch), Texas A&M (1/16 to 1/5 inch) and Penn
  State (about 1/10 inch), each attributed.
- greendrains "the only permanent solution" is a seal: UC IPM, UMN, Texas A&M and Penn State put scrubbing the film out,
  or a bacterial or enzyme drain product, first; UC IPM says regular enzymatic cleaning may prevent the buildup.
- greendrains "a direct health code violation in most jurisdictions" (unsourced; Wikipedia's myiasis and asthma notes
  are not homeowner guidance either): UC IPM says drain flies do not bite, cause no damage, could carry germs where
  sterility matters and may form allergens when dead; that is what our page says.
- Popular advice to pour boiling water or bleach: UC IPM calls it a common misconception (at best short-term),
  UMN says it is not effective, Kentucky says hot water gives short-term control and the larvae are hard to drown.
