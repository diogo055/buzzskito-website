## What our page does that none of them do
1. Real, credited photos of the termite and each look-alike where people find them: swarmers on a building
   wall (Missouri, April 2026), a winged larger yellow ant, a booklouse beside a pen tip, a lyctus beetle on
   split wood, an old house borer larva in its tunnel, beetle holes in a floorboard. None of the three shows a
   booklouse, a beetle larva or damage; Bob Vila's are stock.
2. A "where did you find it?" sorter (windowsill and lights, basement floor cracks, damp wood with shavings,
   wood with mud, hardwood floors and furniture, attic framing, firewood, books and damp rooms), each with
   the one check that confirms it.
3. The one magnifier check that sorts all of them (antennae: straight and beaded / elbowed / long and
   threadlike; plus hard wing covers for beetles) and an 11-row quick-ID table with sourced sizes.
4. Look-alikes extension sources name that the top 3 miss: larger yellow (citronella) ants swarming up through
   basement floors (Ohio State HYG-2064: "often mistaken for winged termites"), booklice (UC Davis Bohart Museum
   and Colorado State: they resemble minute/miniature termites), old house borer larvae, and pale grubs vs
   termite workers inside wood.
5. Every size and claim linked to an extension, USDA Forest Service or EPA source; 10 FAQs.

## What they get wrong (and the source that says otherwise)
- Bob Vila: "About 45 species of termites are found in the U.S." The USDA Forest Service Wood Handbook (ch. 14,
  2021) says about 56 species are known in the United States.
- Bob Vila: white bugs that look like termites are "most likely termites" because few insects are as pale.
  Colorado State and the Bohart Museum (UC Davis) both say pale booklice resemble tiny termites.
- Bob Vila and Lawn Love: flying ants "don't cause any damage". Carpenter ants swarm too and excavate wood
  (Penn State); the Wood Handbook says in certain regions they can do as much damage as termites.
- Lawn Love: acrobat ants have a "stinger". Clemson HGIC 2400 says they bite on occasion and mentions no sting.
- Lawn Love: compares swarmers on diet ("feed on wood"). The workers do the eating (K-State MF3291: workers
  feed on the wood; swarmers fly, shed wings and found colonies).
- Moxie: flying termites are "1/4-3/8 inch" in the intro and "1/4-3/4 inch" in the body. Purdue HN-61 gives
  about 1/4 to 3/8 inch; Penn State 3/8 to 1/2 inch for eastern subterranean reproductives.
- Bob Vila and Moxie list mayflies and green lacewings with no extension source; we found none that names
  them as termite look-alikes, so our page leaves them out. Same for earwigs and white cockroaches.
