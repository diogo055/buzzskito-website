- Lab repellency, Flor-Weiler et al. 2022 (USDA ARS), Exp Appl Acarol 86(2):299-312: "Repellency of CWO against I.
  scapularis nymphs was 94%" (10 min at 33 µg/cm²; 90% at 30 min, 80% at 60 min); "The repellency of CWO on tick species
  decreased with time"; "the repellency of CWO can be as effective as DEET on I. scapularis nymphs". Doses in µg/cm²,

  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24690252/
- On human skin, Burtis et al. 2024 (CDC), 11 minimum-risk tick repellents, 9 of them with cedarwood oil at 1-10%

## Claims on our page checked
1. **"Cedar oil has mild tick-repellent properties — research has confirmed this in lab settings"** (FAQ, l.28; l.195
   adds "tick-killing"). PARTLY TRUE. Lab repellency is confirmed (Flor-Weiler 2022), and it was strong for 10-60 minutes,
   not "mild"; it fades with time, and on skin 11 minimum-risk repellents (9 with cedarwood oil) fell, pooled, to 2%
   repellency by 2.5 h (Burtis 2024). Killing: Eller 2014 showed it with cedrol, a compound in the oil;
   Flor-Weiler 2022 also ran toxicity tests on the oil (results not quoted here). No field trial in a yard found. Fix: "In lab tests cedarwood
   oil repels blacklegged tick nymphs, but the effect fades within hours, and no published field trial shows it lowers
   ticks in a yard."
2. **"Lab studies use 5–10% cedar oil. Many consumer sprays are 1–2%."** (l.197). NOT VERIFIABLE / misleading. The lab
   studies dose in µg/cm² or mg/ml, not percent. Products: 1-10% in skin repellents (CDC table), 12.63% on the
   Wondercide yard-spray label, 84.7% Cedarcide concentrate diluted to about 0.7-1.3% (our arithmetic). The CDC study saw
   no link between concentration and how long a product worked. Fix: drop both percentages.
3. **Re-apply cedar sprays "every 2–4 weeks" (l.28), "every 14 days" (l.40), "every 2 weeks" / "bi-weekly" (l.101, 120,
   125, 143, 157, 172, 199, 201).** FALSE against the makers' directions: Cedarcide "twice, two weeks apart, and then just
   monthly after that"; Wondercide "twice within 2-10 days", then "every 30-45 days or as needed". Neither interval is
   independently tested. Fix: give the makers' intervals as their claims, add that no independent test shows how long
   either keeps ticks down (CAES: organic products "breakdown rapidly").
4. **"Permethrin-based granular pesticides ... hold 4–6 weeks"** (l.100; also l.40, 133, 171). NOT VERIFIABLE, and not
   buyable here: no permethrin granular product and no domestic granular product with ticks on the label is registered in
   Canada. No source gives 4-6 weeks for permethrin granules (Cedarcide's cedar-chip granules carry the same "4–6 weeks").
   "Wide retailer availability" (l.133) is false for Canada; "(e.g. Bayer Advanced)" (l.171) not verified. Fix: reframe
   the "Best Granular" pick as not available in Canada; cite CAES on liquids vs granules.
5. **"Consumer DIY tick sprays typically last only 1–14 days"** (l.102). NOT VERIFIABLE (no source; the same line sits on
   /blog/best-tick-control-yard-treatment). Sources split by product type: synthetic turf insecticides, one application
   usually enough (CAES); pyrethrin + soap, "little residual activity", at least two applications (CAES); garlic, 37-59%
   for 6-18 days; two natural acaricides under 90% at 7 days. Fix: state by product type, with sources.
6. **"Keep grass under 4 inches" (l.177), "Mow grass to 3–4 inches" (l.206).** NOT VERIFIABLE on CDC, PHAC or CAES, which
   give no height: "Mow the lawn frequently" (CDC), "Mow the lawn often to keep the grass short" (PHAC), "Keep grass
   mowed" (CAES). Fix: "mow often, keep it short".
7. **"3-foot wood-chip or gravel barrier"** (l.103, 179, 208). TRUE (CDC "3-ft wide"; CAES "3-foot or wider"). Canada's
   wording: "at least 1 metre wide", wood chip, mulch or gravel (PHAC). Suggest "at least 1 metre (about 3 ft)".
8. **"Ticks don't cross dry, sun-exposed barriers"** (l.208) and "a dry wood-chip strip is a barrier they will not cross"
   (l.213). FALSE as stated. CDC: "to restrict tick migration"; CAES: "may help reduce tick abundance on the lawn,
   although results vary widely"; PHAC: ticks "can't survive for long in dry, sunny areas". Fix: "A dry strip cuts the
   number of ticks that reach the lawn; it does not stop them."
9. **"Health Canada does not approve permethrin liquids and sprays for consumers to treat their own clothing"** (l.148,
   157). TRUE (PHAC page above). But the pick it supports is Insect Shield, which has no Canadian registration; the
   registered apparel is NO FLY ZONE (PCP 32757). "Factory treatment lasts up to 70 wash cycles" (l.151) is a maker claim
   (Insect Shield, unopened; No Fly Zone "up to 70 home launderings"); PHAC gives no wash count. Fix: recommend registered
   treated clothing with a PCP number, people over 16.

Also seen (not on the brief's list):
- "Safe around kids and pets" (Cedarcide pros, l.142): absolute-safety claim; the maker itself says "family-safe, and
  pet-friendly when used as directed"; EPA does not review these products and Health Canada has not registered them.
- "Real, research-backed cedar-oil repellency" (l.124), "the leading evidence-backed cedar oil options" (l.201): the
  evidence is lab and skin-bioassay only (see 1).
- "Garlic spray — Marketing claims dramatically exceed evidence" (Tier 4, l.187): a field trial of a garlic-juice
  product measured 37-59% control for 6-18 days, and a garlic-juice yard repellent with ticks on its label (MOSQUITO
  BARRIER, PCP 32049) is registered in Canada, which includes Health Canada's check that it is "effective for its stated
  purpose". Editor's call.
- "Granular pesticides from Home Depot" (FAQ, l.24, l.48) and the meta description's "permethrin granules": no domestic
  granular tick product is registered in Canada.
- "Discourage deer access (they're the primary tick host)" (l.181): CAES says deer are "the principal host for the adult
  stage of the tick". Minor.
