# Cluster: ticks

## New pages in this cluster (link targets: use these exact addresses)

- `/blog/what-do-ticks-look-like` — **what do ticks look like**. Identification of ticks in general: eight legs (six as larvae), no wings or antennae, size by life stage in everyday comparisons and inches, unfed versus engorged, then the main US species and their markings (blacklegged, western blacklegged, American dog, lone star, brown dog, Gulf Coast, Rocky Mountain wood). A table. Brief on look-alike bugs: link to that page.

- `/blog/seed-ticks` — **seed ticks** (also: baby ticks). Seed ticks are larval ticks (the page also answers 'baby ticks', so explain larvae with six legs versus nymphs with eight): what they look like, why they attack in clusters, which species, how to remove dozens at once, what disease risk larvae and nymphs carry according to CDC, bites, and prevention.

- `/blog/ticks-that-carry-lyme-disease` — **ticks that carry lyme disease picture**. Only two US ticks transmit Lyme disease: the blacklegged tick in the East and Upper Midwest and the western blacklegged tick on the Pacific coast. How to recognize each life stage (searchers want pictures, so include several media placeholders with precise descriptions), which common ticks do not carry Lyme, where the risk is, how long a tick must be attached according to CDC, and what to do after a bite.

- `/blog/ticks-in-michigan` — **ticks in michigan**. Ticks in Michigan using the state health department's own material: the five most common species, where blacklegged ticks and Lyme risk are established and spreading by county, the season, the diseases reported in the state, how to submit a tick for identification through the state program, and prevention.

- `/blog/ticks-on-dogs` — **ticks on dogs**. Ticks on dogs: where to check, what an attached tick looks and feels like versus a skin tag or scab, how to remove one, the diseases dogs get from ticks in the US, how preventives work in general terms (ask a veterinarian), and what to watch for afterward. The hub for dog tick questions: link to the existing dog tick pages and to the instant-kill page.

- `/blog/what-kills-ticks-on-dogs-instantly` — **what kills ticks on dogs instantly**. An honest answer: nothing safely kills an attached tick 'instantly', and fast removal with fine-tipped tweezers is the quickest fix. Then what veterinary products do and how quickly their labels say they start working (cite FDA or EPA and the label, do not invent speeds), the FDA's notice on isoxazoline products, what never to use (alcohol, petroleum jelly, a hot match, essential oils, dish soap claims), and yard measures. Product classes and brand names editorially, no links.

- `/blog/what-kills-ticks` — **what kills ticks**. What kills ticks, by where they are: on clothing (high-heat dryer times from CDC), on gear (0.5 percent permethrin treatment), on skin (remove, do not smother), in the yard (acaricide classes used by label, tick tubes, habitat changes, what university research shows), and the popular things that do not (drowning in the wash, freezing weather, essential oils).

- `/blog/do-chickens-eat-ticks` — **do chickens eat ticks**. Chickens, guinea fowl and other animals credited with eating ticks: yes they eat some, what the published studies actually measured, why that does not add up to tick control for a yard, the opossum claim and what later research found, and what does reduce ticks.

- `/learn/tick-identification-chart` — **tick identification chart**. A comparison chart of the three ticks people most need to tell apart: the blacklegged (deer) tick, the American dog tick and the lone star tick. For each: adult female, adult male and nymph size with an everyday size comparison and inches, the markings that identify it, the diseases CDC says it can transmit, where it lives in the United States, and its range in Ontario and the rest of Canada (cite the Government of Canada or Public Health Ontario for the Canadian range). A size-scale table by life stage. What to do with a tick you have removed, and how to get it identified. A printable tick-check routine card: where on the body to look, in order. This page may name Ontario and Canada: it serves both countries.
